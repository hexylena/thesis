\section{Themes}

This thesis explored pairs of scientific and technical application papers. For each pair we developed new methods or conducted investigations, and in a related piece of work we made that process more findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable, open, and reproducible.

This process was applied on multiple scales of investigaion; from the genomics of relatively uncomplicated prokaryotes (\cref{chapter:annotation}), through a more complex and clinically relevant case involving a pair of human cell types and their use in a hospital (\cref{chapter:clinic}), before finally reflecting on the wider world and the dissemination and sustainability of the knowledge in the first two chapters (\cref{chapter:training}).

Throughout this thesis topics of ensuring accessibility, reducing barriers, ensuring FAIRness, improving user-friendliness, mandating reproducibility, and increasing efficiencies through both automation and process changes have been applied.


\subsection{In the Cell}

As discussed in \cref{introduction} and \cref{chapter:annotation}, high quality genome annotation is of particular importance as a foundational building block for downstream analyses. In the case of bacteriophages, with consideration to their applications in the clinic, we require well annotated genomes\cite{Nilsson_2014,Philipson_2018}. In the world of bacteriophage annotation there are a number of options such as RAST\cite{Overbeek_2013}, or DNAMaster\cite{Pope_2017}, or the NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipline (PGAP)\cite{Tatusova_2016}. However, each of these annotation pipelines have drawbacks which must be evaluated before their application in the clinic. RAST and PGAP both offer very hands-off pipelines, while tools like DNAMaster take the opposite approach and require hands-on annotation with little opportunity for automation. In many disciplines this hands-off approach where a workflow can complete an analysis end-to-end is appropriate and good, but in genome annotation it begets a significant array of problems\cite{Danchin_2018,Ramsey_2021,Li_2012,Drabkin_2012}. Often annotators refer to the ``garbage in garbage out'' problem in annotation. Limited by the lack of financial ability and time to produce knockouts of every gene individually, we must rely on homology and domains for inferring our annotations from related organisms. Annotators often rely on related organisms and annotations of highly conserved genes in other records, to inform annotation within their own organism, when they assign putative names or functions to each gene. If those source annotations are poor or of dubious provenance, automatically copying those annotations based on homology or domain similarity simply spreads bad data. The Evidence Code Ontology\cite{Chibucos_2014} is one attempt to address this issue, by enabling annotation of data sources with code relating to where and how that evidence was obtained. This enables researchers to eliminate data sources they feel do not have sufficiently stringent evidence, trusting those that provide strong evidence for their data, and perhaps enabling more automation in the future. However, the gold standard for genome annotation remains \emph{human-curated} genome annotation, where a flesh and blood human evaluates the results presented to them. This is where a combined system like Galaxy and Apollo (\cref{chapter:phages}) shines; both the portions of the annotation process which can be safely automated, and the portions which require human review can be performed in a collaborative evidence-based fashion, retaining provenance along the way. Apollo integrates the Evidence Code Ontology within its annotation system, allowing annotators to prevent the further spread of poorly defended annotations, and enabling annotators to document their annotation decisions.

Notably, Galaxy and Apollo are both members of the Generic Model Organism Database (GMOD)\cite{Stein_2002}, a community of projects that enable hosting a model organism database (MOD) for a community. Several other related projects are also part of GMOD, enabling groups to deploy full featured MODs such as Chado\cite{Mungall_2007} which provides a generic and reusable database schema, and Tripal\cite{Spoor_2019} which provides a web-based interface to access data within Chado. Apollo and Galaxy both are able to interface with Chado and Tripal, providing a coherent and re-usable set of systems to meet the needs of different organism annotation groups.

In the first theme of genome annotation (\cref{chapter:annotation}), we presented a scalable, real-time collaborative, FAIR platform for annotation that meets the needs of both educational and research contexts. This work leveraged existing, open source, community-maintained software components (Apollo, Galaxy) under the GMOD umbrella, in order to deliver a series of tools and workflows which enhance their integration and interoperability. We optimized this framework through the lens of phage annotation and education; we produced a suite of tools including both novel developments and integration of existing open-source bioinformatics tools. We constructed workflows which combine all of these tools to enable the structural and functional annotation of bacteriophages. This unified interface to annotation enabled the publication of over 160 complete, annotated genome sequences of phages since going into operation, supporting all steps of the process from sequencing to GenBank submission preparation.

This development provides a useful framework for any annotation group working on a model organism, as their needs are often not met by large databases like NCBI or Ensembl which do not necessarily have the in-house experience that species-specific consortia such as FlyBase\cite{Thurmond_2018} or WormBase\cite{Davis_2022} have.
Both of these consortia are members of the Alliance of Genome Resources\cite{2024} which are examples of GMOD-based infrastructure being (re)used to great effect across organisms.
Furthermore, we showed that our approach is also scalable to different ends of existing axes: many small organisms, ras well as fewer large ones. All of these infrastructure components can be hosted publicly or privately, making them a good fit for sensitive healthcare data use cases.
Hospitals could consider leveraging this for hosting internal annotation and variant databases, for human data or pathogenic viruses, and providing standardised infrastructure collecting the wealth of yet unanalysed data generated under their auspices. This will enable better in-house exchange, visualisation, and analysis of data. Post-pandemic some GMOD components have been discussed as possible options for aggregating and sharing outbreak data but the real-world implementation remains to be seen.

A related problem when publishing an annotated genome is providing informative visualisaion to help the reader understand the data collected about this sequence. Common tooling for this includes Circos\cite{Krzywinski_2009} (and related circular plotters like GenoVi\cite{Cumsille_2023}, TBTools\cite{Chen_2022}, Genome U-Plot\cite{Gaitatzes_2017}). While Circos itself is infinitely configurable, going as far as allowing the user to dynamically program through the use of Perl\cite{wall1994perl} code, this complete freedom can provide significant barriers for a newcomer. The related circular plotters mentioned, of which these are only a small subset, take the opposite approach and demand specific input datasets (genome, BLAST results, etc.) and generally have a very fixed output which is often hard to customise. However, they do offer a useful lesson; that providing structured input can improve the accessibility of tooling for newcomers and reduce barriers.

We developed tooling to make the process of creating such publication-quality plots -as one would make for an annotated genome- more accessible. Our Galactic Circos wrapper significantly simplifies the user experience of leveraging Circos for complex multi-dimensional data visualisation. We built upon the lessons of community resources by attempting to find an ideal middle ground between user-friendly but inflexible plotters, and highly flexible but complex-to-use tools. Within Galaxy, the simplified interface of Galactic Circos removes the complexity barrier, allowing more genome annotators and genome visualisers to build advanced and highly customized plots as a part of their genome annotation workflows. The addition of our web-based interface to Circos, as well as related tooling, in Galaxy, completely eliminates the need to install software or have computational space to run such a plotter. Additionally, it enables the exchange of plotting workflows via the community; everyone is able to customise Circos to their liking, and indeed import and modify the plotting workflows that others have shared. This maximises the flexibility by providing near complete access to Circos, while minimising barriers to access, solving the plotting problem.

With tooling and infrastructure built around a foundation of prokaryotic genomics in a single human annotator setting, the focus now turns to applying this methodology to higher order organisms and larger use cases like multiple eukaryote cell lines and larger groups such as hospitals.

\subsection{In the Clinic}

In \cref{chapter:clinic} we turn our attention to large and more complex organisms, namely human cell lines and their data processing. In eukaryotes with vasculatures (e.g. Mus musculus, Homo sapiens), endothelial cells and pericytes form a synergistic relationship to regulate capillary permeability with wide ranging effects---from their involvement blood-brain barrier\cite{Netzahualcoyotzi_2024} to their involvement in tumour microenvironments\cite{Jiang_2023}, or their mutual interactions with pericytes promoting endothelial cell survival\cite{T_r_k_2021}. A better understanding of the vasculature will enable us to more precisely deliver drugs to the correct targets, or conversely to restrict the vasculature to starve out a tumour \cite{Ten_Hagen_2008,Seynhaeve_2007}. There is a lot of existing research regarding the interactions between endothelial cells and pericytes, as well as separately the impact of Tumour Necrosis Factor (TNF) on each. TNF-$\alpha$ is known to affect the permeability of the pericyte membrane\cite{Seynhaeve_2006,Kerkar_2006}, as well induce structural changes within pericytes. However, until now there has been little exploration of TNF's effects on pericytes, much less the effect of endogenous factors produced by pericytes on their neighbouring endothelial cells.

Building on the techniques and of the tools from the first set of investigations, an investigation was conducted into the effects of TNF on both cell types to determine changes in their morphology, mortality, and gene expression.
Additionally an initial exploration of how endogenous factors of TNF stimulated pericytes affect endothelial cells was conducted. While TNF has minimal physical effects on endothelial cells and pericytes, an indirect effect of factors produced by pericytes when exposed to TNF results in increased permeability within endothelial cells.
Within the cells it was observed that cytokines and chemokines were significantly upregulated in both cell types, with pericytes expressing them at a significantly higher than endothelial cells.
This was likewise reflected in the upregulation of Interferon signalling and other inflammatory pathways, observed in both cell types treated with TNF as well as endothelial cells under pericyte conditioned medium suggesting an important role for pericytes in the inflammatory response process.
This suggests the possibility for manipulating the tumour microenvironment in ways that will be beneficial to TNF-induced delivery of drugs.

While in this case the data was not sensitive, this is rather unusual in a hospital setting. With clinically relevant data one often encounters issues of sensitive data, data that cannot be shared outside of the hospital. Projects with these needs require access to sensitive clinical variants from services like the Beacon Network, or human genome data from places like the European Genome Archive (EGA). Currently solutions to these problems often look like hospital IT policies heavily restricting computational access to data, rewriting software to attempt implementation of homomorphic encryption or differential privacy\cite{Mohammed_Yakubu_2019}, negotiating data access agreements with specific vendors promising secure data analysis and encrypted environments\cite{Carter_2019,Tang_2016}, or international collaborations to develop new frameworks which then have to be (re)implemented in enough places to gain critical mass\cite{Rehm_2021}. Others are exploring highly controlled and integrated platforms which provide a secure environment and pre-configured resources at the cost of flexibility and duplication of administration efforts\cite{Schatz_2022}.

To simplify the analysis of sensitive human data, allowing researchers to focus on the analysis rather than the detailed legalities of data storage and analysis, a transparent and simplified manner was developed which enables federated access to data stored in the EGA via a GA4GH compliant method. This new method significantly reduces barriers for researchers when working with such sensitive data, by answering the GDPR question for a number of relevant facets for the researcher, enabling them to spend less time focused on ensuring compliance and more on their analysis. This work does not attempt to solve the global issues of data privacy, nor can it. Instead GA4GH compliant access to EGA attempts to work together with others and implement one portion of a secure data framework and work towards a common solution.

With this research and research utility built in a hospital via analysis of RNA sequencing data and with consideration to researchers working on more sensitive datasets, the focus turns to how to share and best disseminate this knowledge such that groups outside of one hospital can make use of it.

\subsection{In the World}

Finally, \cref{chapter:training} investigates how to disseminate the previously completed work, and how can all of the aforementioned improvements and discoveries be made sustainable. Looking outside of the institute, outside of the work completed to see how it can be best shared with the world, one's focus \emph{must} be turned towards training. Training the previous, the current, and the next generation of researchers in best practices and the availability of new useful tools that can improve their research.

There are a number of popular training platforms in existence such as The Carpentries\cite{Wilson_2006}, EMBL-EBI's Training Platform\cite{Cook_2018,Swan_2024}, and the Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics's (SIB) Training Site\cite{2015}. As with every other implementation each takes a different approach with different trade-offs. The Carpentries and SIB both rely on having course materials in separate code repositories which allows for fine grained access control and a level of dynamic response that enables modifications necessary for individual courses without affecting other potential concurrent users. EMBL-EBI instead leverages an existing content management system and centralises all of the content in one site. Each of these platforms has made incredible strides towards ensuring their training materials are FAIR\cite{Garcia_2020}, and each makes different choices and trade-offs in that quest.

In \cref{chapter:gtn} the improvements to the Galaxy Training Network\cite{gtn2,GTN_web}, a well-used FAIR training platform, are described. During this development cycle the platform added coding interactive tutorials available as automatically generated Jupyter notebooks, automatic video generation from existing slide decks, and explored the potential for future automatic generation of videos from learning materials. All of the implemented features which aim to reduce the burden of maintenance on educators and those wishing to disseminate their results, while increasing the FAIRness of the results. Simultaneously this framework explored improvements the accessibility of the content, in all senses, for learners by providing a freely accessible, learning platform that integrates well with Galaxy, and is accessible for those using a screenreader, e.g. due to a visual impairment. These explorations showed that it is not only possible but even easy to implement a FAIR and truly accessible platform for learning materials. Unlike other platforms, GTN learning resources obtain a perfect score of FAIRness\cite{GTN_fairchecker} via FAIR-Checker\cite{Gaignard_2023}. Receiving regular screen-reader testing, it is perhaps one of the most accessible platforms for those with disabilities.
% to ensure that it meets accessibility best practices to ensure content distributed via this channel is accessible to those with visual or hearing impairments, that provides a framework for educators to collaboratively manage peer-reviewed learning materials.

With all of the resources and tooling produced, all of the FAIR training materials collected on the GTN, it isn't sufficient to stop there. The concrete implementation of training is still necessary, and still a large barrier impacting teachers globally, namely in the obtaining of training infrastructure. There are a few programs offering ``cloud credits'' such as NIH's Cloud Lab program\cite{cloud-lab} which leave instructors with the problem of building their own training infrastructure atop the generic cloud. However, at least in the Galaxy-adjacent training space, where trainers are used to freely accessible Galaxy servers funded by local grants, there is still the desire and need to have access to priority queue access for efficient training. On an orthogonal axis, outside of monitored training environments such as classrooms where the teacher can always see the students' screens, there is currently no software which provides instructor visibility into the progress of students.

The resolution of these issues was explored via the implementation of an open-source training infrastructure sharing platform, Training Infrastructure as a Service (TIaaS), which provided a method for system administrators to more easily offer training infrastructure for educators. This saw 24k learners use the platform over its 5 years of existence, approximately 75\% of which used materials hosted on the aforementioned GTN. The reduction of barriers in educators providing courses was deeply felt based on the feedback received, the complexity of managing infrastructure was previously one of the most painful points of organising an event, completely removed leveraging the combination of Galaxy and TIaaS and the GTN. Additionally, the TIaaS service enables instructors to monitor the progress of their class in an anonymous manner, enabling more efficient and streamlined classes while balancing the privacy of individual students.

\section{Future Perspectives}

\subsection{Reproducibility}

FAIR data has been a wonderful achievement, where it has been achieved. FAIR non-data resources (code, documents, learning materials) likewise. However, FAIR, in fact, does not require the permanent accessibility of data, only the \emph{meta}data. Reproducibility is also not part of the acronym, although ``FAIRR'' has been coined for situations and some authors interpret the original FAIR standards as requiring reproducibility\cite{Lamprecht_2020}.

This is not sufficient; stronger \emph{standards} are necessary for both data and computational methods that require not just metadata preservation, but \emph{data} preservation. Likewise, the software executed in order to determine some result must be likewise preserved. Given the current Reproducibility Crisis in the scientific world\cite{Baker_2016}, the best defence against this is sunlight; placing our results in the open and making them easier to reproduce. This also means the results will be easier to re-analyse and re-review, ensuring that inconsistencies can be identified and incorrect calculations corrected.

Many of these practices are feasible today. On one hand a lack of software development education for researchers deeply impacts their ability to deliver high quality software as software development is not seen as an output of the scientific process. On the other hand, the capitalistic ``Publish or Perish'' ecosystem focused only on publishable results, and the funding ecosystem which actively harms long term maintenance of projects, leaves only voluntary compliance to ensure that we hold ourselves to a sufficient standard. And ``[T]he problem with voluntary programs is that not everybody volunteers''\cite{csb-safety}.

Regulators, journals, and funding agencies must be pushed to implement minimum standards and mandates. A relatively small change such as a journal requirement has the potential for a significant impact on the reproducibility, longevity, and FAIR-ness of scientific research.

\subsection{Software Development Best Practices}

While no one expects a biologist or bench scientist coming from a non-software engineering background to know all or any of the field's best practices, they should be a necessary and core part of developing research software for publication. When writing scripts or tools or building pipelines, especially in a clinical setting, meeting these best practices should become the bare minimum for publication. The best practices developed by the software industry are a response to an intent to decrease defects within their outputs, and we as a clinical research (software) development community should seek to emulate them. The use of version control systems, extensive continuous testing and integration, typed languages, and other measures exist for increasing software correctness. All of these tools to improve software quality spring from a place of need within software producing communities and address real problems that exist with the casual use of code to solve problems.

Some of the onus is on the developers of Scientific Workflow Management Systems (SciWMSs) to guide the user in meeting those best practices, Galaxy is a good example here with both graphical user interface and command line interface based linting of workflows to help researchers implement best practices. An additional part of the onus should be on journals and funding agencies, to place intentional barriers in the way of researchers requiring that the software they develop is published, licensed correctly, using a reproducible dependency management system. However the rest lies with the researcher, to ensure that a published result can be reproduced.

% Software isn't made in isolation, we are all part of this community. For instance, many scientific outputs, even the simplest scripts, can have complex dependency trees. The developer must consider the reproducibility guarantees; perfect reproducibility requires vendoring of dependencies, if requirements are less strict then perhaps pinning and hashing is sufficient. Many software dependency systems are vulnerable to so called ``left-pad incidents''\cite{enwiki:1223519004}, where a single dependency, when revoked, has significant ramifications throughout the dependency tree, and software will fail to execute because that dependency no longer exists on the internet. If that is part of the threat model, then vendoring is required.

It might seem strange to argue for the intentional placement of barriers, after spending so much effort to reduce them. This is in fact completely coherent; barriers that inhibit quality science should be removed, barriers that ensure that bare-minimum level of quality should be reinforced. A plausible result is that these barriers will decrease scientific software availability; more data is required but it is certainly no worse of a situation than ``code available upon request'' emails sent to the graduate student that left a decade ago.

Software isn't made in isolation, we are all part of this community, and it's on the entire community to push themselves and their collaborators and colleagues to use best practices. These practices should be disseminated from those of us with the knowledge, to those with the funding to implement them in their software.

\subsection{Accessibility}

Accessibility for those with impairments or disabilities is an issue close to the author's heart, but much of the work done globally is inaccessible. The Social Model\cite{Oliver_2013} of disability suggests to us that disability is not an individual problem to rectify, rather an opportunity for us to improve in ways that accommodate \emph{everyone}. The classic example is a ``dropped kerb''\footnote{en\_US: Curb Cut, en\_AU: Kerb Ramp}, whereby a change made in infrastructure to provide continuous access from street to sidewalk for someone making use of a wheelchair has potentially previously unconsidered benefits for others such as a parent with a pram. Parents no longer needs to move the pram the 10-15cm up and down on every street crossing, nor are individual expensive wheelchairs required which could navigate high curbs.

The same accessibility must be considered in the work that is produced as a by-product of the scientific process. A large number of websites and tools and occasionally even documentation and training materials are generated as by-products of the scientific process. If the scientific community wants to value all perspectives and inputs, it must make these outputs more accessible. This can be as simple as ensuring the colours are accessible for someone with colour blindness ($\approx$4\% of the human population) via using a colour-blind friendly palette for visualisations and graphics, or checking for sufficient contrast (a problem that increases with age, or is noticeable for anyone reading on a screen in the sun, or anyone with low toner in their printer) using automated tools like WAVE\cite{wave}. For figures and graphics published online this means ensuring that they have sufficient alternative text or ``alt text'', so those using a screenreader do not miss out on a key result that an author is attempting to communicate. Currently essentially no journals accomplish this, except for a handful of disability specific journals\cite{Crane_2023}. It is an area that is extremely fruitful for improvements going forward, a reactant we can add to the synthesis of results, that will have an impact on the entire system. Publication figure alt text would enable authors to explicitly communicate the major conclusions contained within a graphic, providing both a check for a reviewer that a graphic is line with their understanding of the publication and its conclusions, as well as a way for visually impaired readers to still obtain the meaning of the figure and its implications.

In a similar manner as the dropped kerb, the world of cognitive impairments has related issues and easily implemented solutions. Both those impairments occurring in permanent manners (e.g. due to ageing), or temporary ones (a concussion from sports, a poor night's sleep due to a crying baby), ranging from mild distractedness to severe comprehension or memory issues, many people experience a range of cognitive impairments throughout their life. The software development world offers many solutions and ameliorations that can be applied, such as thoroughly documenting code, writing architecture documents, or developing tutorials to ensure others can easily become proficient in its use. However, these must be done thoroughly, one form of documentation isn't always sufficient. Sufficient documentation not only helps one easily restart work on a project after a temporary distraction (e.g. a grant that needed to be submitted), but also helps other users of the software to know it exists and learn how to use it, across language and cultural barriers.

It might be the best analysis pipeline in the world, but if potential users cannot operate it, what was the point in building it? If it is the best pipeline, positively impacting healthcare, then there is a moral imperative to maximise the impact via improved available.

\subsection{Hospital \textit{Systems}}

System administrators who are responsible for large amounts of infrastructure, such as hospital systems, are often informed by systems theory and systems safety.

In ICT and infrastructure management this view is useful as it gives one new ways to solve problems. Is a system overloaded? One \emph{could} provide more servers, more storage, and resources to the problem; but one could also look at other changes like queueing, rate-limiting requests, changing how the software interacts with the system, or changing the software itself! All of these, \emph{and more}, are valid approaches that may produce the desired result and possibly more efficiently. Being responsible for critical systems\footnote{The definition of criticality is interesting to bear in mind; a system may not be considered mission critical by the organisation, but its users may disagree as some part of the critical chain of their work would be completely stopped were a service to not be available. Most systems are critical to someone. As services and software are born into existence by research work and grant funded labour, it must not be done carelessly, without any thoughts to the future: future maintenance, sustainability, and preservation.} that people depend on also forces one to consider systems safety; \emph{when}, not if, a system will fail, how can it be ensured that it does so with the least amount of collateral damage? How can we reduce the amount of fallout for clinicians, researchers, and patients, while simultaneously keeping patient data secure?

In the context of bioinformatics, this provides a new lens through which to analyse the sum total of what is produced, and how it is produced. Within this complex, interdependent, networked system known as research, there are many human interdependencies; colleagues are producing data, some analysing it, and others implementing changes based on the results. Additionally, there are the relationships between humans and software, software and hardware, all interconnected and interdependent. In this system there are many opportunities for making changes which have downstream effects; flow rates that can be adjusted to change the overall process or efficiency. But the correct ones must be identified, the small changes that will have outsized results, to most efficiently improve the system. Simultaneously systems safety cannot be forgotten. When steps in a process inevitably fail, such as pipelines or tools, can they fail safely and in ways that minimise negative effects?

Looking to bioinformatics in a hospital, one finds a system incredibly focused on producing new clinical applications, occasionally to the exclusion of everything else. The incremental progress made towards better clinical impacts benefits humanity, but maintaining the current focus is potentially globally suboptimal; the same results could benefit a far larger group, if only a broader interpretation of the hospital's mission was used, one which places value on ancillary aspects such as software development, software safety, training, and dissemination.

However, ``the purpose of a system is what it does''; a hospital heals patients, it does not develop software.

\section{Conclusion}

In this thesis multiple themes in the world of Bioinformatics were explored, from the annotation of a single organism, through their exploration in the clinic, and finally to the dissemination of the knowledge obtained throughout the first two arcs. FAIR and Open Source Software and Open Science principles were applied throughout the development process to ensure the quality and interoperability and accessibility of our results.

This work has already impacted an incredibly large number of individuals around the world, multiple orders of magnitude more than the typical bioinformatics software release. More focus should be given to these surrounding aspects of the bioinformatics process, rather than simply tool development, as they can provide a force multiplier for the tool development and developers they work with collaboratively. Building a dissemination and training platform, and providing training infrastructure, has ensured that tools and software that would be otherwise unknown, are not only findable, but also accessible, (re)usable, and reproducible for a global audience. Ensuring that the work that was done, was accessible for all, has guaranteed that a diverse community can make use of what was produced for years to come.
