System administrators who are responsible for large amounts of infrastructure,
are often informed by systems theory and systems safety.

In infrastructure management this view is useful as
it gives one new ways to solve problems. Is a system overloaded? One \emph{could} provide 
more expensive resources to the problem and scale horizontally or vertically, but one could also look at other changes like
queueing, rate-limiting requests, changing how the software interacts with the
system, or changing the software itself! All of these, \emph{and more}, are valid
approaches that may produce the desired result and possibly more efficiently.
Being responsible for critical systems\footnote{The definition of criticality is interesting to bear in mind; a system may not be considered mission critical by the organisation, but its users may disagree as some part of the critical chain of their work would be completely stopped were a service to not be available. Most systems are critical to someone. As services and software are born into existence by research work and grant funded labour, it must not be done carelessly, without any thoughts to the future: future maintenance, sustainability, and preservation.}
that people depend on
also forces one to consider systems safety; \emph{when}, not if, a system will
fail, how can it be ensured that it does so with the least amount of collateral
damage? The least amount of fallout for users? 

In the context of bioinformatics, this provides a new lens through which to analyse the sum total of what is produced, and how it is produced.
Within this complex, interdependent, networked system known as research, there are many human interdependencies; colleagues are producing data, some analysing it, and others implementing changes based on the results. 
Additionally there are the relationships between humans and software, software and hardware, all interconnected and interdependent.
In this system there are many opportunities for making changes which have downstream effects; flow rates that can be adjusted to change the overall process or efficiency.
But the correct ones must be identified, the small changes that will have outsized results, to most efficiently improve the system.
Simultaneously systems safety cannot be forgotten. 
When steps in a process inevitably fail, such as pipelines or tools, can they fail safely and in ways that minimise negative effects?

Looking to bioinformatics in a hospital, one finds a system incredibly focused on
producing new clinical applications, occasionally to the exclusion of everything else.
The incremental progress made towards better clinical impacts benefits humanity,
but maintaining the current focus is potentially globally suboptimal;
the same results could benefit a far larger group, if only the core mission was expanded to include elevating ancillary aspects of the process.

\section{Themes}

This thesis explored pairs of scientific and technical application papers; we developed new methods or conducted an investigation, and, in a related piece of work, we make part of that process more findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable, and reproducible.

This process was repeated across two major themes---the genome of a single organism, and a more complex and clinically relevant cell types---before finally reflecting on dissemination and sustainability of this knowledge.

Throughout this thesis topics of ensuring accessibility, reducing barriers, ensuring FAIRness, improving user-friendliness, mandating reproducibility, and increasing efficiencies through both automation and process changes have been explored.

\subsection{In the Cell}

In the first theme of genome annotation (\cref{chapter:annotation}), a scalable, real-time collaborative, FAIR platform for annotation was presented that meets the needs of both educational and research contexts.
This work leveraged existing, open source, community maintained software components (Apollo, Galaxy) both of which exist under the Generic Model Organism Database (GMOD) umbrella, in order to deliver a series of tools and workflows which enhance their integration and interoperability.
This framework was examined through the lens of phage annotation and education; a suite of tools was produced via novel developments and integration of existing open source bioinformatics tools, and workflows constructed which aggregated all of those tools to enable the structural and functional annotation of bacteriophages.
This unified interface to annotation enabled the publication of over 160 %\footnote{\url{https://scholar.google.com/scholar?start=40&q=\%7Bhttps://cpt.tamu.edu/galaxy-pub/+complete+genome+sequence}}
complete, annotated genome sequences of phages since going into operation, supporting all steps of the process from sequencing to GenBank submission preparation.

Subsequently the process of making the production of publication quality plots, such as one would make for an annotated genome, more accessible was investigated via the development of the Galactic Circos wrapper which significantly simplified the user experience of leveraging Circos for complex multi-dimensional data visualisation.
Circos presented a significant accessibility barrier in terms of the time required to become proficient in its operation due the complex interface it supports, the simplified interface removes that barrier allowing more genome annotators and genome visualisers to build complex plots as a part of their genome annotation workflows.

\subsection{In the Hospital}

Moving from less complicated organisms like bacteriophages and prokaryotes, to an RNA-Seq analysis of two experiments with Endothelial cells and Pericytes in \cref{chapter:cancer},
the interactions of these two cells with Tumor Necrosis Factor was investigated. Significant upregulation of both CXCL and CCL family of cytokines were observed under the experimentatl condition of TNF. This was likewise reflected in the upregulation of Interferon signaling and other inflammatory pathways which was observed in both cells treated with TNF as well as endothelial cells under pericyte conditioned medium suggesting an important role for pericytes in the inflammatory response process.

In order to simplify the analysis of sensitive human data, allowing researchers to focus on the analysis rather than the detailed legalities of data storage and analysis, a transparent and simplified manner was developed which enables federated access to data stored in the European Genome Archive (EGA) via a GA4GH compliant method. This new method significantly reduces barriers for researchers when working with such sensitive data, by answering the GDPR question for a number of relevant facets for the researcher, enabling them spend less time focused on ensuring compliance and more on their analysis.

\subsection{Out in the World}

Finally \cref{chapter:training} investigates how to disseminate the previously completed work, and how can all of the aforementioned improvements and discoveries be made sustainable.

The first paper shows the multi-year set of improvements to the Galaxy Training Network, a well used FAIR training platform. % to ensure that it meets accessibility best practices to ensure content distributed via this channel is accessible to those with visual or hearing impairments, that provides a framework for educators to collaboratively manage peer-reviewed learning materials.
During this development cycle the platform added coding interactive tutorials available as automatically generated Jupyter notebooks, automatic video generation, a comprehensive linting framework, and explored the potential for future automatic generation of videos from learning materials.
All of the implemented features which aim to reduce the burden of maintenance on educators and those wishing to disseminate their results, while increasing the FAIRness of the results.
Simultaneously this framework explored improvements the accessibility of the content, in all senses, for learners by providing a freely accessible, learning platform that integrates well with Galaxy, and is accessible for those using a screenreader, e.g. due to a visual impairment.
These explorations showed that it is not only possible but even easy to implement a FAIR and truly accessible platform for learning materials.

The application thereof was explored via the implementation of an open source training infrastructure sharing platform, Training Infrastructure as a Service (TIaaS), which provided a method for system administrators to more easily offer training infrastructure for educators. This saw 24k learners use the platform over its 5 years of existence, approximately 75\% of which used materials hosted on the aforementioned GTN. The reduction of barriers in educators providing courses was deeply felt based on the feedback received, the complexity of managing infrastructure was previously one of the most painful points of organising an event, completely removed leveraging the combination of Galaxy and TIaaS and the GTN.

\section{Future Perspectives}

\subsection{Reproducibility}

FAIR data has been a wonderful achievement, where it has been achieved. FAIR non-data resources (code, documents, learning materials) likewise. However, FAIR, in fact, does not require the permanent accessibility of data, only the \emph{meta}data. Reproducibility is also not part of the acronym, though ``FAIRR'' is becoming slowly more popular.

Instead stronger standards are necessary for both data and computational methods that requires not just metadata preservation, but \emph{data} preservation. Likewise the software executed in order to determine some result must be likewise preserved. Given the current Reproducibility Crisis in the scientific world, the best defense against this is sunlight; placing our results in the open, and making them easier to reproduce. This also means they will be easier to re-analyse and re-review, ensuring that inconsistencies can be rooted out, and incorrect calculations corrected.

We need to explore containerisation technologies like Singularity and Docker containers to preserve historic operating systems, we need to ensure the software we use is open source, and optimally hosted on an archival site like via the Software Heritage program. We need to campaign with software developers to ensure their tools can be seeded, if any random number generation is used.

Many of these practices are completely feasible today, but due to a lack of software development education for researchers, coupled with the capitalistic ``Publish or Perish'' ecosystem focused only on publishable results, and the funding ecosystem which actively harms long term maintenance of projects, leaves only voluntary compliance to ensure that we hold ourselves to a sufficient standard.

\subsection{Software Development Best Practices}

While no one expects a biologist or bench scientist coming from a non-software engineering background to know all of the field's best practices, it needs to be considered a core responsibility if they are writing scripts or tools or building pipelines, especially in a clinical setting.
Software development best practices as defined here implies topics such as peer-review of code, version control systems, testing and correctness, performance analysis, language typing, ``parse don't validate'', Postel's Law, software licensing, etc.

Some of the onus is on the developers of Scientific Workflow Management Systems (SciWMSs) to guide the user in meeting those best practices, Galaxy is a good example here with both UI and CLI based linting of workflows to help researchers implement best practices.
An additional part of the onus should be on journals and funding agencies, to place intentional barriers in the way of researchers requiring that the software they develop is published, licensed correctly, using a reproducible dependency management system.

Software isn't made in isolation, even the simplest scripts very often have complex dependency trees.
The developer must consider the reproducibility guarantees; perfect reproducibility required demands vendoring of dependencies, if requirements are less strict then perhaps pinning and hashing is sufficient.
Many software dependency systems are vulnerable to so called ``left-pad incidents'', where a single dependency, when revoked, has significant ramifications throughout the dependency tree, and software will fail to execute because that dependency no longer exists on the internet.

It might seem strange to argue for the intentional placement of barriers, after spending so much effort to reduce them. 
This is in fact completely coherent; barriers that inhibit quality science should be removed, barriers that ensure that level of quality should be reinforced.
A plausible result is that these barriers will decrease scientific software availability, more data is required but it is certainly no worse of a situation than ``code available upon request'' emails sent to the graduate student that left a decade ago.

\subsection{Accessibility}

Accessibility for those with impairments or disabilities is an issue close to the author's heart, but much of the work done globally is inaccessible.
The Social Model of disability suggests to us that disability is not an individual problem to rectify, rather an opportunity for us to improve in ways that accommodate \emph{everyone}.
The classic example is a ``dropped kerb''\footnote{AmEng: Curb Cut, AusEng: Kerb Ramp}, whereby a change made in infrastructure to provide continuous access from street to sidewalk for someone making use of a wheelchair has potentially not previously considered benefits for others such as a parent with a pram.
The parent no longer needs to move the pram the 10-15cm up and down on every street crossing, nor are individual expensive wheelchairs which could navigate high curbs.

The same accessibility must be considered in the work that is produced as a by-product of the scientific process.
A large number of websites and tools and occasionally even documentation and training materials are generated and if the scientific community wants to value all perspectives and inputs, it must make more accessible materials.
This can be as simple as ensuring the colours are accessible for someone with colour blindness ($\approx$4\% of the population) via using a colour-blind friendly palette for visualisations and graphics, or checking for sufficient contrast (a problem that increases with age, or is noticeable for anyone reading on a screen in the sun, or anyone with low toner in their printer) using automated tools like WAVE.
For figures and graphics published online this means ensuring that they have sufficient alternative text or ``alt text'', so those using a screenreader do not miss out on a key result that an author is attempting to communicate.
Currently essentially no journals accomplish this, except for a handful of disability specific journals.
It is an area that is extremely fruitful for improvements going forward, a reactant we can add to the synthesis of results, that will have an impact on the entire system.
Publication figure alt text would enable authors to explicitly communicate the major conclusions contained within a graphic, providing both a check for a reviewer that a graphic is line with their understanding of the publication and its conclusions, as well as a way for visually impaired readers to still obtain the meaning of the figure and its implications.

In a similar manner as the dropped kerb, the world of cognitive impairments has related issues and easily implemented solutions.
Both in permanent manners (e.g. due to ageing), or temporary ones (a poor night's sleep due to a crying baby), ranging from mild distractedness to severe comprehension or memory issues, many people experience a range of cognitive impairments throughout their life.
The software development world offers many solutions and ameliorations that can be applied, such as thoroughly documenting code, writing architecture documents, or developing tutorials to ensure others can easily become proficient in its use.
Sufficient documentation not only helps one easily restart work on a project after a temporary distraction (say a grant that needed to be submitted), but also helps other users of the software to know it exists and learn how to use it, across language and cultural barriers.
It might be the best analysis pipeline in the world, but if a potential user cannot operate it, what was the point in building it?
If it is the best pipeline positively impacting healthcare, there is a moral imperative to make it as widely available as possible.

\section{Conclusion}

In this thesis multiple themes in the world of Bioinformatics were explored, from the annotation of a single organism, through their exploration in the clinic, and finally to the dissemination of the knowledge obtained throughout the first two arcs. FAIR and Open Source Software and Open Science principles were applied throughout the development process to ensure the quality and interoperability and accessibility of our results.

This work has already impacted an incredibly large number of individuals around the world, multiple orders of magnitude more than the typical bioinformatics software release. 
More focus should be given to these surrounding aspects of the bioinformatics process, rather than simply tool development, as they can provide a force multiplier for the tool development and developers they work with collaboratively.
Building a dissemination and training platform, and providing training infrastructure, has ensured that tools and software that would be otherwise unknown, are not only findable, but also accessible, (re)usable, and reproducible for a global audience.
Ensuring that the work that was done, was accessible for all, has guaranteed that a diverse community can make use of what was produced for years to come.
