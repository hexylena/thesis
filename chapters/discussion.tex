\section{Themes}

This thesis explored pairs of scientific and technical application papers; we developed new methods or conducted an investigation, and, in a related piece of work, we make part of that process more findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable, and reproducible.

This process was explored across a spectrum of size; from the genomics of uncomplicated prokaryotes (\cref{chapter:annotation}), through to the genomics of a more complex and clinically relevant pair of human cell lines and their use in a single hospital (\cref{chapter:cancer}), before finally reflecting on the wider world and the dissemination and sustainability of the knowledge in the first two chapters (\cref{chapter:training}).

Throughout this thesis topics of ensuring accessibility, reducing barriers, ensuring FAIRness, improving user-friendliness, mandating reproducibility, and increasing efficiencies through both automation and process changes have been explored.

\subsection{In the Cell}

As discussed in \cref{introduction} and \cref{chapter:annotation}, high quality genome annotation is of particular importance as a foundational building block for downstream analyses. In the case of bacteriophages, with consideration to their applications in the clinic, we required well annotated genomes\cite{Nilsson_2014,Philipson_2018}. In the world of bacteriophage annotation there are a number of options such as RAST\cite{Overbeek_2013}, or DNAMaster\cite{Pope_2017}, or the NCBI PGAP\cite{Tatusova_2016}. However, each of these pipelines have drawbacks which must be considered before their application. Both RAST and PGAP offer very hands-off pipelines, while tools like DNAMaster take the opposite approach and requires hands-on annotation with little opportunity for automation. In many disciplines this hands-off approach where a workflow can complete an analysis end-to-end is appropriate and good, but in genome annotation it begets a significant array of problems\cite{Danchin_2018}. This is often called the ``garbage in garbage out'' problem in annotation; limited by the lack of financial ability and time to produce knockouts of every gene individually, we must rely on homology and domains for inferring our annotations from related organisms. Annotators often rely on related organisms and annotations of highly conserved genes in other records, to inform annotation within their own organism, when they assign putative names or functions to each gene. But who annotated that other organism? Did they have the same standards? The Evidence Code Ontology\cite{Chibucos_2014} is one attempt to address that, by enabling annotation of data sources with code relating to where and how that evidence was obtained. This can enable researchers to eliminate data sources they feel do not have sufficiently stringent evidence, trust those that provide strong evidence for their data, and perhaps enable more automation in the future. However, the gold standard for genome annotation remains \emph{human-curated genome annotation}, which means we must have a human-in-the-loop step where they analyse and interpret the results available to them. This is where a combined system like Galaxy and Apollo (\cref{chapter:phages}) shine, together the portions which can be safely automated, and the portions which require human review can be done in a collaborative evidence-based fashion. Apollo notably integrates the Evidence Code Ontology within its annotation system, allowing annotators to prevent the further spread of poorly defended annotations.

Notably, Galaxy and Apollo are both members of the Generic Model Organism Database (GMOD)\cite{10.1101/gr.403602}, a community of projects that enable hosting a model organism database (MOD) for a community. Several other related projects are part of GMOD which enable groups to deploy a full featured MODs such as Chado\cite{10.1093/bioinformatics/btm189} providing a generic and reusable database schema, Tripal\cite{10.1093/database/baz077} providing a web-based interface to access data within Chado. Apollo and Galaxy both are able to interface with Chado and Tripal, providing a coherent and re-usable infrastructure to meet the needs of different organism annotation groups.

In the first theme of genome annotation (\cref{chapter:annotation}), a scalable, real-time collaborative, FAIR platform for annotation was presented that meets the needs of both educational and research contexts. This work leveraged existing, open source, community-maintained software components (Apollo, Galaxy) under the GMOD umbrella, in order to deliver a series of tools and workflows which enhance their integration and interoperability. This framework was examined through the lens of phage annotation and education; a suite of tools was produced via novel developments and integration of existing open-source bioinformatics tools, and workflows constructed which aggregated all of those tools to enable the structural and functional annotation of bacteriophages. This unified interface to annotation enabled the publication of over 160 complete, annotated genome sequences of phages since going into operation, supporting all steps of the process from sequencing to GenBank submission preparation.

This development provides a useful framework for any annotation group working on a model organism, as their needs are often not met by large databases like NCBI or Ensembl which do not necessarily have the in-house experience that species specific consortia have, such as FlyBase\cite{10.1093/nar/gky1003} or WormBase\cite{10.1093/genetics/iyac003}. Both of these are members of the Alliance of Genome Resources\cite{10.1093/genetics/iyae049} which are examples of GMOD-based infrastructure being used to great effect across organisms. In the Phage work it was shown that it is also scalable on different ends of existing axes: many, small organisms, rather than a few large ones. All of these infrastructure components can be hosted publicly or privately, making them a good fit for sensitive healthcare data use cases. Hospitals could consider leveraging this for hosting internal annotation and variant databases, for human data or viruses, and providing standardised infrastructure collecting the wealth of yet unanalysed data generated under their auspices enabling better in-house exchange, visualisation, and analysis of data. Post-pandemic some GMOD components have been discussed as possible options for aggregating and sharing outbreak data but their implementation remains to be seen.

A related problem when publishing an annotated genome is providing a nice figure to help the reader understand the data collected about this sequence. Common tooling for this includes Circos\cite{Krzywinski_2009} (and related circular plotters like GenoVi\cite{Cumsille_2023}, TBTools\cite{Chen_2022}, Genome U-Plot\cite{Gaitatzes_2017}). While Circos itself is infinitely configurable, going as far as allowing the user to dynamically program through the use of Perl code\cite{wall1994perl}, this complete freedom can provide a barrier for a newcomer. The related circular plotters mentioned, of which those are only a small subset, take the opposite approach and demand specific input datasets (genome, BLAST results, etc.) and generally have a very fixed output which is often hard to customise. However, they do offer a useful lesson; that providing structured input can improve the accessibility of tooling for newcomers and reduce barriers. The addition of a web-based interface provided ``for free'' by Galaxy, enabled further removing barriers present in other competing genome plotting tools: the need to install software.

The process of making such publication quality plots, as one would make for an annotated genome, more accessible was investigated via the development of the Galactic Circos wrapper which significantly simplified the user experience of leveraging Circos for complex multi-dimensional data visualisation. Circos presented a significant accessibility barrier in terms of the time required to become proficient in its operation due the complex interface it supports, the simplified interface removes that barrier allowing more genome annotators and genome visualisers to build complex plots as a part of their genome annotation workflows. By providing Circos itself, and the related tooling in Galaxy, a simple web interface that can be used for free is provided to the world, completely eliminating the need to install software or have computational space to run such a plotter. Additionally, it enables the exchange of plotting workflows via the community; everyone is able to customise Circos to their liking, and indeed modify the plotting workflows of others that are shared. This maximises the flexibility by providing near complete access to Circos, while minimising barriers to access, perfectly solving the plotting problem.

With tooling and infrastructure built around a foundation of prokaryotic genomics in a single human annotator setting, the focus now turns to applying those to higher order organisms and larger use cases like multiple eukaryote cell lines and larger groups such as hospitals.

\subsection{In the Clinic}

In eukaryotes with vasculatures (e.g. Mus musculus, Homo sapiens), endothelial cells and pericytes form a synergistic relationship to regulate capillary permeability with wide ranging effects---from their involvement blood-brain barrier\cite{Takata_2011} to their involvement in tumour microenvironments\cite{Jiang_2023}. A better understanding of the vasculature will enable us to more precisely deliver drugs to the correct targets, or conversely to restrict the vasculature to starve out a tumour \cite{Ten_Hagen_2008}. There is a lot of existing research regarding the interactions between ECs and PCs, as well as separately the impact of TNF on each. TNF-$\alpha$ is known to affect the permeability of the PC membrane\cite{Kerkar_2006}, as well induce structural changes within PCs. However, until now there has been little exploration of TNF's effects on ECs and PCs, much less the effect of TNF-exposed ECs on PCs.

Building on the techniques and of the tools from the first investigation it was necessary to investigate the effects of TNF of ECs and PCs, and their potential effects of TNF-exposed ECs on PCs, to further investigate their signalling behaviour\cite{Matsumoto_2014}. Significant upregulation of both CXCL and CCL family of cytokines were observed under the experimental condition of TNF. This was likewise reflected in the upregulation of Interferon signalling and other inflammatory pathways which was observed in both cells treated with TNF as well as endothelial cells under pericyte conditioned medium suggesting an important role for pericytes in the inflammatory response process.

While in this case the data was not sensitive, this is rather unusual in a hospital setting. With clinically relevant data one often encounters issues of sensitive data, data that cannot be shared outside of the hospital. Projects with these needs require access to sensitive clinical variants from services like the Beacon Network, or human genome data from places like the European Genome Archive (EGA). Currently solutions to these problems often look like hospital IT policies heavily restricting computational access to data, rewriting software to attempt implementation of homomorphic encryption or differential privacy\cite{Mohammed_Yakubu_2019}, negotiating data access agreements with specific vendors promising secure data analysis and encrypted environments\cite{Carter_2019,Tang_2016}, or international collaborations to develop new frameworks which then have to be (re)implemented in enough places to gain critical mass\cite{Rehm_2021}. Others are exploring highly controlled and integrated platforms which provide a secure environment and pre-configured resources at the cost of flexibility\cite{Schatz_2022}.

To simplify the analysis of sensitive human data, allowing researchers to focus on the analysis rather than the detailed legalities of data storage and analysis, a transparent and simplified manner was developed which enables federated access to data stored in the EGA via a GA4GH compliant method. This new method significantly reduces barriers for researchers when working with such sensitive data, by answering the GDPR question for a number of relevant facets for the researcher, enabling them to spend less time focused on ensuring compliance and more on their analysis. This work does not attempt to solve the global issues of data privacy, nor can it. Instead GA4GH compliant access to EGA attempts to work together with others and implement one portion of a secure data framework and work towards a common solution.

\subsection{In the World}

Finally, \cref{chapter:training} investigates how to disseminate the previously completed work, and how can all of the aforementioned improvements and discoveries be made sustainable. Looking outside of the institute, outside of the work completed to see how it can be best shared with the world, one's focus must be turned towards training.

There are a number of popular training platforms in existence such as The Carpentries\cite{Wilson_2006}, EMBL-EBI's Training Platform\cite{Swan_2024}, and the Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics's (SIB) Training Site. As with every other implementation each takes a different approach with different trade-offs. The Carpentries and SIB both rely on having course materials in separate code repositories which allows for fine grained access control and a level of dynamic response that enables modifications necessary for individual courses without affecting other potential concurrent users. EMBL-EBI instead leverages an existing content management system and centralises all of the content in one site. Each of these platforms has made incredible strides towards ensuring their training materials are FAIR\cite{Garcia_2020}, and each makes different choices and trade-offs in that quest.

In \cref{chapter:gtn} the improvements to the Galaxy Training Network\cite{gtn2,GTN_web}, a well-used FAIR training platform, are described. During this development cycle the platform added coding interactive tutorials available as automatically generated Jupyter notebooks, automatic video generation, a comprehensive linting framework, and explored the potential for future automatic generation of videos from learning materials. All of the implemented features which aim to reduce the burden of maintenance on educators and those wishing to disseminate their results, while increasing the FAIRness of the results. Simultaneously this framework explored improvements the accessibility of the content, in all senses, for learners by providing a freely accessible, learning platform that integrates well with Galaxy, and is accessible for those using a screenreader, e.g. due to a visual impairment. These explorations showed that it is not only possible but even easy to implement a FAIR and truly accessible platform for learning materials. Unlike other platforms, GTN learning resources obtain a perfect score of FAIRness\cite{GTN_fairchecker} via FAIR-Checker\cite{Gaignard_2023}.
% to ensure that it meets accessibility best practices to ensure content distributed via this channel is accessible to those with visual or hearing impairments, that provides a framework for educators to collaboratively manage peer-reviewed learning materials.

With all of the resources and tooling produced, all of the FAIR training materials collected on the GTN, it isn't sufficient to stop there. The concrete implementation of training is still necessary, and still a large barrier impacting teachers globally, namely in the obtaining of training infrastructure. There are a few programs offering ``cloud credits'' such as NIH's Cloud Lab program\cite{cloud-lab} which leave instructors with the problem of building their own training infrastructure atop the generic cloud. However, at least in the Galaxy-adjacent training space, where trainers are used to freely accessible Galaxy servers funded by local grants, there is still the desire and need to have access to priority queue access for efficient training. On an orthogonal axis, within the training space outside of monitored classrooms where the teacher can always see the students' screens, there is currently no software which provides instructor visibility into the progress of students.

The application thereof was explored via the implementation of an open-source training infrastructure sharing platform, Training Infrastructure as a Service (TIaaS), which provided a method for system administrators to more easily offer training infrastructure for educators. This saw 24k learners use the platform over its 5 years of existence, approximately 75\% of which used materials hosted on the aforementioned GTN. The reduction of barriers in educators providing courses was deeply felt based on the feedback received, the complexity of managing infrastructure was previously one of the most painful points of organising an event, completely removed leveraging the combination of Galaxy and TIaaS and the GTN. Additionally, the TIaaS service enables instructors to monitor the progress of their class, enabling more efficient and streamlined classes.

\section{Future Perspectives}

\subsection{Reproducibility}

FAIR data has been a wonderful achievement, where it has been achieved. FAIR non-data resources (code, documents, learning materials) likewise. However, FAIR, in fact, does not require the permanent accessibility of data, only the \emph{meta}data. Reproducibility is also not part of the acronym, though ``FAIRR'' is becoming slowly more popular.

Instead, stronger standards are necessary for both data and computational methods that require not just metadata preservation, but \emph{data} preservation. Likewise, the software executed in order to determine some result must be likewise preserved. Given the current Reproducibility Crisis in the scientific world, the best defence against this is sunlight; placing our results in the open and making them easier to reproduce. This also means the results will be easier to re-analyse and re-review, ensuring that inconsistencies can be identified and incorrect calculations corrected.

Many of these practices are feasible today. Due to a lack of software development education for researchers, coupled with the capitalistic ``Publish or Perish'' ecosystem focused only on publishable results, and the funding ecosystem which actively harms long term maintenance of projects, leaves only voluntary compliance to ensure that we hold ourselves to a sufficient standard.

``[T]he problem with voluntary programs is that not everybody volunteers''\cite{csb-safety}.

We must push for regulators, journals, and funding agencies to implement minimum standards and mandates. A relatively small change such as a journal requirement has the potential for a significant impact on the reproducibility crisis.

\subsection{Software Development Best Practices}

While no one expects a biologist or bench scientist coming from a non-software engineering background to know all or any of the field's best practices, however these need to be considered a core responsibility if they are writing scripts or tools or building pipelines, especially in a clinical setting. Software development best practices as defined here implies topics such as peer-review of code, version control systems, testing and correctness, performance analysis, language typing, ``parse don't validate'', Postel's Law, software licensing, etc.

Some of the onus is on the developers of Scientific Workflow Management Systems (SciWMSs) to guide the user in meeting those best practices, Galaxy is a good example here with both graphical user interface and command line interface based linting of workflows to help researchers implement best practices. An additional part of the onus should be on journals and funding agencies, to place intentional barriers in the way of researchers requiring that the software they develop is published, licensed correctly, using a reproducible dependency management system.

Software isn't made in isolation, even the simplest scripts very often have complex dependency trees. The developer must consider the reproducibility guarantees; perfect reproducibility required demands vendoring of dependencies, if requirements are less strict then perhaps pinning and hashing is sufficient. Many software dependency systems are vulnerable to so called ``left-pad incidents'', where a single dependency, when revoked, has significant ramifications throughout the dependency tree, and software will fail to execute because that dependency no longer exists on the internet.

It might seem strange to argue for the intentional placement of barriers, after spending so much effort to reduce them. This is in fact completely coherent; barriers that inhibit quality science should be removed, barriers that ensure that level of quality should be reinforced. A plausible result is that these barriers will decrease scientific software availability, more data is required but it is certainly no worse of a situation than ``code available upon request'' emails sent to the graduate student that left a decade ago.

\subsection{Accessibility}

Accessibility for those with impairments or disabilities is an issue close to the author's heart, but much of the work done globally is inaccessible. The Social Model of disability suggests to us that disability is not an individual problem to rectify, rather an opportunity for us to improve in ways that accommodate \emph{everyone}. The classic example is a ``dropped kerb''\footnote{AmEng: Curb Cut, AusEng: Kerb Ramp}, whereby a change made in infrastructure to provide continuous access from street to sidewalk for someone making use of a wheelchair has potentially not previously considered benefits for others such as a parent with a pram. The parent no longer needs to move the pram the 10-15cm up and down on every street crossing, nor are individual expensive wheelchairs which could navigate high curbs.

The same accessibility must be considered in the work that is produced as a by-product of the scientific process. A large number of websites and tools and occasionally even documentation and training materials are generated and if the scientific community wants to value all perspectives and inputs, it must make more accessible materials. This can be as simple as ensuring the colours are accessible for someone with colour blindness ($\approx$4\% of the population) via using a colour-blind friendly palette for visualisations and graphics, or checking for sufficient contrast (a problem that increases with age, or is noticeable for anyone reading on a screen in the sun, or anyone with low toner in their printer) using automated tools like WAVE. For figures and graphics published online this means ensuring that they have sufficient alternative text or ``alt text'', so those using a screenreader do not miss out on a key result that an author is attempting to communicate. Currently essentially no journals accomplish this, except for a handful of disability specific journals. It is an area that is extremely fruitful for improvements going forward, a reactant we can add to the synthesis of results, that will have an impact on the entire system. Publication figure alt text would enable authors to explicitly communicate the major conclusions contained within a graphic, providing both a check for a reviewer that a graphic is line with their understanding of the publication and its conclusions, as well as a way for visually impaired readers to still obtain the meaning of the figure and its implications.

In a similar manner as the dropped kerb, the world of cognitive impairments has related issues and easily implemented solutions. Both in permanent manners (e.g. due to ageing), or temporary ones (a poor night's sleep due to a crying baby), ranging from mild distractedness to severe comprehension or memory issues, many people experience a range of cognitive impairments throughout their life. The software development world offers many solutions and ameliorations that can be applied, such as thoroughly documenting code, writing architecture documents, or developing tutorials to ensure others can easily become proficient in its use. Sufficient documentation not only helps one easily restart work on a project after a temporary distraction (say a grant that needed to be submitted), but also helps other users of the software to know it exists and learn how to use it, across language and cultural barriers. It might be the best analysis pipeline in the world, but if a potential user cannot operate it, what was the point in building it? If it is the best pipeline positively impacting healthcare, there is a moral imperative to make it as widely available as possible.

\subsection{Hospital \textit{Systems}}

System administrators who are responsible for large amounts of infrastructure, such as hospital systems, are often informed by systems theory and systems safety.

In ICT and infrastructure management this view is useful as it gives one new ways to solve problems. Is a system overloaded? One \emph{could} provide more servers, more storage, and resources to the problem; but one could also look at other changes like queueing, rate-limiting requests, changing how the software interacts with the system, or changing the software itself! All of these, \emph{and more}, are valid approaches that may produce the desired result and possibly more efficiently. Being responsible for critical systems\footnote{The definition of criticality is interesting to bear in mind; a system may not be considered mission critical by the organisation, but its users may disagree as some part of the critical chain of their work would be completely stopped were a service to not be available. Most systems are critical to someone. As services and software are born into existence by research work and grant funded labour, it must not be done carelessly, without any thoughts to the future: future maintenance, sustainability, and preservation.} that people depend on also forces one to consider systems safety; \emph{when}, not if, a system will fail, how can it be ensured that it does so with the least amount of collateral damage? How can we reduce the amount of fallout for clinicians, researchers, and patients, while simultaneously keeping patient data secure?

In the context of bioinformatics, this provides a new lens through which to analyse the sum total of what is produced, and how it is produced. Within this complex, interdependent, networked system known as research, there are many human interdependencies; colleagues are producing data, some analysing it, and others implementing changes based on the results. Additionally, there are the relationships between humans and software, software and hardware, all interconnected and interdependent. In this system there are many opportunities for making changes which have downstream effects; flow rates that can be adjusted to change the overall process or efficiency. But the correct ones must be identified, the small changes that will have outsized results, to most efficiently improve the system. Simultaneously systems safety cannot be forgotten. When steps in a process inevitably fail, such as pipelines or tools, can they fail safely and in ways that minimise negative effects?

Looking to bioinformatics in a hospital, one finds a system incredibly focused on producing new clinical applications, occasionally to the exclusion of everything else. The incremental progress made towards better clinical impacts benefits humanity, but maintaining the current focus is potentially globally suboptimal; the same results could benefit a far larger group, if only a broader interpretation of the hospital's mission was used, one which places value on ancillary aspects such as software development, software safety, training, and dissemination.

However, ``the purpose of a system is what it does''; a hospital heals patients, it doesn't develop software.

Perhaps in the future that can change.

\section{Conclusion}

In this thesis multiple themes in the world of Bioinformatics were explored, from the annotation of a single organism, through their exploration in the clinic, and finally to the dissemination of the knowledge obtained throughout the first two arcs. FAIR and Open Source Software and Open Science principles were applied throughout the development process to ensure the quality and interoperability and accessibility of our results.

This work has already impacted an incredibly large number of individuals around the world, multiple orders of magnitude more than the typical bioinformatics software release. More focus should be given to these surrounding aspects of the bioinformatics process, rather than simply tool development, as they can provide a force multiplier for the tool development and developers they work with collaboratively. Building a dissemination and training platform, and providing training infrastructure, has ensured that tools and software that would be otherwise unknown, are not only findable, but also accessible, (re)usable, and reproducible for a global audience. Ensuring that the work that was done, was accessible for all, has guaranteed that a diverse community can make use of what was produced for years to come.
