Many system administrators who are responsible for large amounts of infrastructure,
are informed by systems theory and systems safety.

In infrastructure management this view is useful as
it gives one new ways to solve problems. Is a system overloaded? One \emph{could} throw
more resources at the problem, but one could also look at other changes like
queueing, rate-limiting requests, changing how the software interacts with the
system, changing the software itself! All of these, \emph{and more}, are valid
approaches that may produce the desired result and possibly more efficiently.
Being responsible for critical, or even non-critical systems, that people depend one,
also forces one to consider systems safety; \emph{when}, not if, a system will
fail, how can it be ensured that it does so with the least amount of collateral
damage?

In the context of bioinformatics, this provides a new lens through which to
analyse the sum total of what is produced, and how it is produced.
% For the past years I have participated in the complex, interdependent, networked system known as \emph{research}.
Within this complex, interdependent, networked system known as research, there are many human interdependencies; colleagues are producing data, some analysing it, and others implementing changes based on the results.
In this system there are many opportunities for making changes which have downstream effects;
flow rates that can adjust that change the overall process.
But the correct ones must be identified, the small changes that will have outsized  results, to most efficiently improve the system.
Simultaneously systems safety cannot be forgotten. When pipelines or
tools fail, can they fail safely and in ways that do not negatively affect
patient outcomes?

Looking to bioinformatics in a hospital, one finds a system incredibly focused on
producing new clinical applications, occasionally to the exclusion of everything else.
The incremental progress made towards better clinical outcomes benefits humanity,
but maintaining the current focus is potentially globally suboptimal.
The same results could benefit a far larger portion of the world if aspects \emph{surrounding} our core mission were considered.

\section{Themes}

This thesis explored pairs of scientific and technical application papers; we developed new methods or investigated something interesting, and, in a related piece of work, we make part of that process more findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable, and reproducible.

This process was then repeated across three themes, starting from the genome of a single cell or organism, moving to complex and clinically relevant organisms, before finally reflecting on dissemination and sustainability of this knowledge.

Throughout this thesis topics of ensuring accessibility, reducing access barriers, ensuring FAIRness, and increasing efficiencies through both automation and process changes have been explored.

\subsection{In the Cell}

In this work a scalable, real-time collaborative, FAIR platform for genome annotation was presented that meets the needs of both educational as well as research contexts.
\cref{chapter:phages} leveraged existing, open source, community maintained software components (Apollo, Galaxy) which both exist under the Generic Model Organism Database (GMOD) umbrella and provided a series of tools which enhances their integration and interoperability.
This was examined through the lens of phage annotation and education; a suite of tools was produced via novel developments and integration of existing open source bioinformatics tools, and workflows constructed which aggregated all of those tools to enable the structural and functional annotation of bacteriophages.
This unified interface to annotation enabled the publication of over 160%\footnote{\url{https://scholar.google.com/scholar?start=40&q=\%7Bhttps://cpt.tamu.edu/galaxy-pub/+complete+genome+sequence}}
complete, annotated genome sequences of phages since going into operation, supporting all steps of the process from sequencing to GenBank submission preparation.

Subsequently making the production of publication quality plots more accessible was investigated via the development of the Galactic Circos wrapper in \cref{chapter:circos} which significantly simplified the user experience of leveraging Circos for complex multi-dimensional data visualisation.
Circos presented a significant accessibility barrier in terms of the time required to become proficient in its operation due the complex interface it supports, the simplified interface removes that barrier allowing more genome annotators and genome visualisers to build complex plots as a part of their genome annotation workflows.

\subsection{Out in the World}

\Cref{chapter:gtn} shows the multi-year set of improvements to the Galaxy Training Network, a well used FAIR training platform. % to ensure that it meets accessibility best practices to ensure content distributed via this channel is accessible to those with visual or hearing impairments, that provides a framework for educators to collaboratively manage peer-reviewed learning materials.
During this development cycle the platform added coding interactive tutorials available as automatically generated Jupyter notebooks, automatic video generation, a comprehensive linting framework, and explored the potential for future automatic generation of videos from learning materials.
All of the implemented features which aim to reduce the burden of maintenance on educators and those wishing to disseminate their results, while increasing the FAIRness of the results.
Simultaneously this framework explored improvements the accessibility of the content, in all senses, for learners by providing a freely accessible, learning platform that integrates well with Galaxy, and is accessible for those using a screenreader, e.g. due to a visual impairment.

The application thereof was explored via the implementation of an open source training infrastructure sharing platform \cref{chapter:tiaas} which provided a method for system administrators to offer training infrastructure for educators. This saw 24k learners use the platform over its 5 years of existence, approximately 75\% of which used materials hosted on the aforementioned GTN. The reduction of barriers in educators providing courses was deeply felt based on the feedback received, the complexity of managing infrastructure was previously one of the most painful points of organising an event, completely removed leveraging the combination of Galaxy and TIaaS and the GTN.

\section{Future Perspectives}

\subsection{Reproducibility}
FAIR data has been a wonderful achievement, where it has been achieved. FAIR non-data resources (code, documents, learning materials) likewise. However, FAIR, in fact, does not require the permanent accessibility of data, only the \emph{meta}data. Reproducibility is also not part of the acronym, though ``FAIRR` is becoming slowly more popular.

Instead stronger standards are necessary for both data and computational methods that requires not just metadata preservation, but \emph{data} preservation. Likewise the software executed in order to determine some result must be likewise preserved. Given the current Reproducibility Crisis in the scientific world, the best defense against this is sunlight; placing our results in the open, and making them easier to reproduce. This also means they will be easier to re-analyse and re-review, ensuring that inconsistencies can be rooted out, and incorrect calculations corrected.

\section{Conclusion}

In this thesis multiple themes in the world of Bioinformatics were explored, from the annotation of a single organism, through their exploration in the clinic, and finally to the dissemination of the knowledge obtained throughout the first two arcs. FAIR and Open Source Software and Open Science principles were applied throughout the development process to ensure the quality and interoperability of our results.

This work has already impacted an incredibly large number of individuals around the world, multiple orders of magnitude more than the typical bioinformatics software release. More focus should be given to these surrounding aspects of the bioinformatics process, rather than simply tool development, as they can provide a force multiplier for the tool development itself. Building a dissemination and training platform, and providing training infrastructure, has ensured that tools and software that would be otherwise unknown, are not only findable, but also accessible, (re)usable, and reproducible for a global audience.
