As an individual that spent many, many years responsible for large amounts of
infrastructure, and likewise as a bioinformatician, I approach a lot of
problems with a ``systems thinking'' view, informed by systems theory, and systems safety.

In infrastructure management this view was useful as it gave me new ways to
solve infrastructure problems. Is a system overloaded? We could add more
resources, but we could also look at other changes like queueing, rate-limiting
requests, changing how the software interacts with the system, changing the
software itself! All of these, \emph{and more}, are valid approaches that may
produce the desired result.
I additionally brought a systems safety viewpoint to my work;
\emph{when}, not if, a system will fail, how can we ensure it does so with the least amount of damage?

In the context of bioinformatics, this gives me a new lens through which to analyse the sum
total of what we produce, and how we produce it. In the complex, interdependent,
networked system of ``research'' we have many levers we can pull, flow rates we
can change, but we need to identify which are the correct ones, and which can
be adjusted with minimal effort to produce outsized results.

Simultaneously we need to keep in mind the safety aspect. When our pipelines
or components fail, can they fail safely and in ways that do not negatively
affect patient outcomes.

\section{Bioinformatics: A Systems Lens}

When we look to clinical bioinformatics we find a system incredibly focused on
producing new software, methods, algorithms, and models.
The incremental progress we make towards better clinical outcomes benefits us
all, of course, but maintaining the focus we have is globally suboptimal.
In the system of bioinformatics, we have far too little focus on the machinery
of this progress; how do these resources get created in the first place, and
what happens after they are created?

What use is it when a resource is created in the forest and no one hears it fall
(or is released online without proper documentation and learning materials?)

\subsection{System Interdependencies}

When one thinks of bioinformatics, one often thinks of the specific silo of
bioinformatics that is interesting to them, perhaps a specific analysis, tool,
service, or method. This focus on a single node in the network of
bioinformatics hides a lot of the complexity and cross-domain interactions that
are not only possible\cite{Gannon_2005} but required\cite{adams2010cross,Doom_2003}.

Linear pathways can be drawn in bioinformatics, from data to analysed product,
or from sequencing to differential expression analysis, but these obscure the
networks of topics and fields and analyses that exist outside of those yellow
brick roads, those happy paths. The reality is that the field of bioinformatics
look more like the most complicated biosynthesis pathway one can imagine. It's
full of interconnections between the traditionally included bioinformatics, and sometimes present
laboratory science\cite{Pucker_2019}, but also to education and didactic theory, data
management planning, bioethics, software engineering practices\cite{Lawlor_2015},
publishing. It is these secondary set of connections that aren't often considered,
but are a prerequisite for functional science and positive outcomes.

Take for instance a typical RNA-Seq analysis, generally this is summarised with
a very simple flowchart like \cref{rnaSeqSimple}. Ask ten bioinformaticians,
you'd probably get this answer every time which, while correct, is not complete.

This simplified view hides a lot of very important aspects of responsible
science\cite{NAS1992} such as where is one running these computations? Is it
reproducible? Where did they learn to do it? Is that resource up to date?
What's the data management plant? Is it ethical?

All of these topics are part and parcel of the scientific process, especially in
biomedical sciences, but too often ignored for a focus on translational research
and clinical applications. So let's look at the ``real'' flow chart of doing a
simple differential expression analysis instead.

\Cref{rnaSeqNotSoSimple} shows a more accurate view of the simple analysis.
Every single aspect of the process, from start to end, builds on learning
materials and resources. When created by professionals, their creation is an
involved and complicated process to produce a high quality learning resource.
Someone, somewhere, needed to learn the requisite skills to complete their
subset of the workflow. Additionally many of the steps are computational in
nature, which requires we ask questions such as which
Operating System (OS), Distributed Resource Manager (DRM), and dependency
management system were used? These are all important for the reproducibility of
the analysis, and are as important as the research results in order to avoid
potential fraud by being able to provide a traceable chain of analyses
that is reproducible now, \emph{and} at a later date.
