\section{404: Help Not Found}
% - bioinformatics is difficult
% - bioinformaticians and not really scalable
% - people need our resources, we need to make them available
% - And we need to make training available
Bioinformatics is a rapidly growing field, but the growth in data is far outpacing the growth in researchers.
Advancements in sequencing mean that we generate NUMBER PB of data every TIME PERIOD which must be analysed.
Unfortunately researchers are not scaling at the same rate as the data.

It is a classic economics problem, the supply of humans to accomplish research is significantly lower than the demand.
The demand continues to grow putting pressure on the supply chain for bioinformaticians, pushing us to find ways to make
bioinformatics, and bioinformaticians, more scalable.

Naturally we look at this problem as a systems problem: what levers are available to us which can change the inputs or
outputs of the system to match our desired goal? We can turn the compute lever, making compute resources more available
to researchers via efforts like the \#EuroHPC JU. Or maybe we should place pressure on making tooling more cost
efficient. Can we increase the supply of students coming in to bioinformatics programs?

It is the last point where we have focused a number of our efforts: increasing and helping to accelerate the supply of
bioinformaticians that are needed around the world. We have made numerous training resources available, and then looked
at how to optimally scale these resources to reach the world.

\section{Accessible Bioinformatics}
% - even when tools exist, accessibility issues are abound
% - two kinds: disability access + availability
% - does a given tool work for a blind user?
% - can it be accessed around the world? in non-LMICs?
When one talks about accessibility, there are two understandings which are often conflated; access to a resource, and
access for those with disabilities. Making bioinformatics accessible, in both senses, should be our goal. How can we
make resources we maintain, available to the entire world, barrier free.

Can we make the compute resources available in High Income Countries (HICs), generally the ex-colonial powers, available
to those in Low and Middle Income Countries (LMICs)? Can we make our carefully guarded knowledge available around the
world, for a reasonable price point? When these resources are available, are we actively excluding this with one or more
disabilities from engaging with the materials and tools?

The answer presented in this thesis is a resounding ``yes, we can do it''. We show the possibility to disseminate
resources in global training events reaching around 7.000 students over the 3 years of my PhD employment. Additionally
it is shown to be possible to cost effectively make these resources accessible to those with visual or auditory
impairments.

\section{Aim of the Thesis}
A lot of our esteemed colleagues have focused on the bioinformatics of cancer
itself as well as clinical solutions to the problem.
We instead aim to focus on the platform, infrastructure, and training aspects of cancer genomics.
This presents a unique opportunity to act as a force multiplier for the field, by making the tools and training
more accessible to the world. With our platform we can scale the important work
of our colleagues reaching tens of thousands of researchers.

While this is not a traditional focus for bioinformatics at a medical
university, it remains an important one. What use are the tools if no one has
heard of them?

\section{Scope of this Thesis}
In chapter \cref{chapter:platform} we present three highly integrated components as a necessary basis for the rest of the thesis. First a platform on which to scale one's research, from learning and courses to full fledge research. Into this platform a scalable and FAIR training platform has been integrated leveraging the connections to optimise the learning experience of students. And lastly, we address the needs of trainers using these two platform components by providing training infrastructure, as a service, reaching 24.000 researchers over the 5 years reported on as of the publication date.
% TODO: maybe I should move the GTN paper to chapter 2?

As the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic isolated the world, we were able to leverage the platform and training infrastructure to rapidly respond to the needs of the community, namely providing an e-learning platform. In chapter \cref{chapter:training} we show that the training platform, alongside Galaxy and TIaaS is scalable and functional even in the face of a sharp transition from in-person to e-learning. We show the platform scales to teach ~7.000 registrants over three years of online courses.

With a truly scalable, FAIR platform in place we next look at the connection to the medical research world, specifically cancer genomics in chapter \cref{chapter:cancer}. We demonstrate this by deploying a number of bioinformatics tools import for Cancer on our platform and making them available globally. This only left us with a problem of where to obtain data, thus necessitating the integration of the European Genome Archive (EGA) in a secure manner, allowing for end-to-end analysis of sensitive clinical data. Lastly we present a clinical application of this platform with the analysis of EC-PC data.

Lastly chapter \cref{chapter:annotation} we look at the annotation of genomes, a necessary step in the analysis of any genome, especially in medical contexts. We start by presenting a platform for scalable, real-time genome annotation, and then demonstrate the use of this platform in the annotation of phages, a topic of growing interest in the medical community. We also present the work of an ontology based toolkit for FAIR community databases, and summarise all of this infrastructure work by making our genome annotation infrastructure easily re-deployable by other groups via the GGA docker containers.
