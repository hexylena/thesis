\section{A Brief History} %<<<

The ``source code of life'' could finally begin to be unravelled for organisms, with the advent of DNA sequencing in the 1970s.
The first organism sequenced was bacteriophage $\phi$X174 (5386 nucleotides long) in 1976, which marked the start of the sequencing race.
The Human Genome Project (HGP) was launched in 1990, resulting in an over 90\% complete genome sequence being made available by the HGP and Celera Genomics by 2003.
These scientific and technological developments paved the way for the era of bioinformatics, and for its subsequent application to translational research and clinical diagnostics.

A new field combining informatics\footnote{For non-European audiences, informatics traces its lineage from \textit{Informatica}, i.e. computer science and computational informatics, rather than simply a meaning like ``data'' or ``information''.} with biological research, Bioinformatics, evolved with genomic data availability.
This new interdisciplinary field would be built upon biology, computer science, chemistry, statistics, and more, leveraging knowledge from all of those fields to analyse (non-)human data.
Computers and algorithms were essential to the field by the 1980s, which resulted in a period of rapid developments.
These included comparative genomics to enable multi-genome analysis, phylogenetics to identify the history of organisms, genome annotation methods to determine what each fragment of DNA does, and the first genomic repositories providing worldwide access to genomic and sequencing data (e.g. GenBank® from National Institutes of Health, and databases at  European Molecular Biology Laboratory).
By a decade later in 1992, these public sequence repositories enabled the development of Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) which let researchers search for DNA sequences across ever-growing databases of genomes.

Software accessibility is a key component in making bioinformatics itself both more accessible and more efficient overall.
Computers themselves are notoriously user unfriendly themselves, so much so that an entire field of research exists, dedicated to user experience and human computer interaction.
All of the infrastructure, tools, and data put in front of a busy doctor, or a researcher, needs to be clear, accessible, and user-friendly.
This requires an investment of time and space in the development cycle to ensure all sharp edges are polished, and the software and research products are optimised for their clinical settings.
The gaps in software interface accessibility must be analysed and simplifications investigated to reduce barriers for both existing bioinformaticians and newcomers.


The European Union's General Data Protection Regular (GDPR)'s passage has increased infrastructure access challenges, requiring organisations and researchers to be more careful about data locality, access control, and developing an understanding of the legalities of processing clinical data in different situations, leading to questions about how to easily access data without compromising security.
Many hospitals mandate use of a prescribed platform for data analysis which often comes with additional barriers or costs to access.
Researchers may need to be physically within the hospital to access their data, or forbidden from analysing it on large external clusters to which they might have access, out of legal concerns.
If they cannot analyse their data on their own machines, they potentially need to find additional budget to pay for access to their employer's in-house secure compute environment.
The gap between researchers and their data analysis needs to be explored, the technical issues separated from the skill issues, and both addressed.

Both of the above challenges are compounded by the need to provide training materials.
No matter how user friendly the systems are, they must still be documented in a variety of ways that everyone interacting with the system will require: documentation for developers, training for clinicians, reference manuals for failure modes.

An underlying theme in the above, while remaining a separate issue, is the accessibility of the solutions that can be developed to the aforementioned issues.
The learning materials, the infrastructure, the tooling, the legal knowledge; all of this must be accessible to bioinformaticians.
There are multiple forms of ``accessibility'' barriers that can be experienced, often conflated under the same word; inaccessibility due to lack of effort to make content and resources accessible to those with disabilities, inaccessibility due to access restrictions like firewalls or paywalls, inaccessibility due to missing metadata or (meta)data not using a standard protocol.
Accessibility issues abound and must be catalogued, discussed, and remedies explored.

\subsection{Data Gaps} %<<<

On the data side similar challenges are experienced relating to insufficient time and resources allocated to their solution. Notably while there exists a wealth of primary data products (e.g. DNA, RNA sequencing), the secondary, downstream data products are a potential future limit. Unfortunately their root cause is often again lack of human bioinformaticians.

Clinical pipelines are exceptional in their data requirements: in the clinical world accurate genome annotation is of paramount importance. This can be seen especially as we look towards reviving old practices such as bacteriophages to fight infections, those phages must have correctly annotated and fully known genomes before they are exposed to humans. Unlike small mRNA-based vaccines where the entire sequence is known, bacteriophages are significantly larger with more unknowns, both due to difficulties in sequencing and time required for human annotators to investigate every coding region. Many healthcare providers are cautious about introducing organisms with unknown genes into a patient; one wants to ensure annotations are human-reviewed for accuracy. Finally, when we look at maintaining high quality curation, we need to find a way to ensure it is sustainable long term as well as this data must be regularly updated.

The same applies to human data as well: before the genetic underpinnings of a disease or condition are fully understood, an annotated genome must be available, the sequencing alone is of course insufficient. In the precision medicine use case this means increasingly working with sensitive human annotation datasets, the challenge becomes how to handle these datasets safely and securely. Long term data storage becomes an important question to answer to ensure documented traceable clinical analysis.
% sensitive data: bench lab scientists need fool-proof ways to access sensitive, secure data to reduce potential for negative outcomes.
% federated data: these researchers also want to look at cohorts from other institutions, other countries in a GDPR safe manner, so we need to enable that.

For population level studies common in oncological research, these datasets are also often no longer in-house, and instead must be obtained from federated sensitive data repositories.
Given the context of the technical and legal problems mentioned above, these problems become significantly more difficult as we look to transferring human data across national boundaries.
For the bioinformatician wishing to analyse those datasets, it might additionally mean using federated compute resources to bring their computations directly to the data.
Infrastructure to support safe and secure cross-national exploration of medical datasets must be investigated and solutions devised which enable federated data access whilst protecting privacy and ensuring security.

\section{Scope of this Thesis} %<<<
In \cref{chapter:annotation} we examine the question of bacteriophages; for education they present an interesting opportunity to improve bioinformatics and genome annotation education, for healthcare they offer an interesting target for phage therapy.
We present a suite of tooling which builds on open source software from the Generic Model Organism Database (GMOD) community to develop a comprehensive platform supporting genome annotation of bacteriophages from education through to production, and consider its utility as a platform for annotation curation and maintenance.
Additionally we explore visualisation of genomic annotation data leveraging Circos for complex multi-dimensional data visualisation, and explore a simplified user interface to a complicated tool that is more accessible for researchers.
All of the produced results are made available on the Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR) data analysis platform that is Galaxy, exploring heterogeneous computational resources and software in a transparent manner.

Next in \cref{chapter:cancer} we investigate clinical applications of sequencing data collected from Endothelial cells and Pericytes to investigate their response to Tumour Necrosis Factor (TNF), paving the way to future implications for treating cancers involving those two cell types.
This work is complemented by the development of a FAIR data retrieval pipeline enabling researchers to more seamlessly access sensitive data for their research projects and analyses, decreasing access barriers while simultaneously maintaining data security for seamless access of federated clinical data.

Finally, with a lot of interesting results in the first two themes, we explore potential solutions for dissemination, ensuring the longevity and sustainability of our work by sharing our techniques through training.
As we benefit at every step from the existing learning materials produced by educators around the world, we give back by building a scalable and FAIR training platform which has seen significant developments over the last two years in \cref{chapter:training}.
We examine potential improvements to the underlying platform, including increasing accessibility of training materials for all tools on the platform, acting as a force multiplier.
This is paired with a significant problem facing trainers: obtaining and configuring infrastructure for training events or courses.
Solutions and methods by which we can decrease barriers and enable re-usability are explored through the implementation of a platform which enables easier access to training infrastructure.
