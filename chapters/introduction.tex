\section{Paying the Bioinformatics TARRIF[sic]}
FAIR has been a key concept in bioinformatics since some years. It is a vital component in making sure our research does
not go to waste. By requiring FAIR data funders help ensure that datasets are:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Findable
	\item Accessible
	\item Interoperable
	\item Reusable
\end{enumerate}

Additionally some have added a second R: Reproducibility. This aims to highlight and address the reproducibility crisis
within science. If we have truly FAIR datasets, we should be able to easily reproduce the research.

I propose the addition of a T: Teaching (or training). It is insufficient to ``throw data over the wall''\footnote{a term often used
to describe software releases of pseudo open source software. The authors are maintaining some source of internal
development and only occasionally sharing their work, when it suits them, resulting in potentially long gaps between
what is released, and what services they offer on their website, impairing reproducibility.}, one must also put forth
the effort of documenting or teaching others how to use these datasets in such a way that can be useful.

What should be made of a dataset for an uncommon research project that cannot leverage community standards and
resources. It might be FAIR, even FAIRR if there is a pipeline generating it, but without documentation and training
materials associated with the resource describing the generation process it becomes prohibitively costly to engage with
an unknown dataset.

\subsection{Teaching/Training}
To make a FAIR resource functional we must train others to access this resource. This dovetails with the requirement for
Reusable semantically, however the requirements for reusable data are quite low: dataset metadata is described, using
common standards (when possible), and with an acceptable license. While those are all useful aspects, we should go
further and document how to make use of our resources.
It can save researchers time when they want to make use of our data.

\section{Beyond FAIR}

FAIR (or TARRIF if you will), set a baseline for the resources we produce as part of our scientific efforts. It it the
minimum, not the maximum. There are areas where we can choose to go above any beyond the minimum requirements for good
science. In some of those areas we can see sufficiently impressive ROIs that make the extra effort expended worth the
cost.

\subsection{Accessibility}

% Not just accessible but easily!
% - even when tools exist, accessibility issues are abound
% - two kinds: disability access + availability
% - does a given tool work for a blind user?
% - can it be accessed around the world? in non-LMICs?

When one talks about accessibility, there are multiple understandings which are often conflated; access to a resource, and
access for those with disabilities. Making bioinformatics accessible, in both senses, should be our goal. How can we
make resources we maintain, available to the entire world, barrier free.

Can we make the compute resources available in High Income Countries (HICs), generally the ex-colonial powers, available
to those in Low and Middle Income Countries (LMICs)? Can we make our carefully guarded knowledge available around the
world, for a reasonable price point? When these resources are available, are we actively excluding this with one or more
disabilities from engaging with the materials and tools?

The answer presented in this thesis is a resounding ``yes, we can do it''. We show the possibility to disseminate
resources in global training events reaching around 7.000 students over the 3 years of my PhD employment. Additionally
it is shown to be possible to cost effectively make these resources accessible to those with visual or auditory
impairments.

\section{404: Help Not Found}
% - bioinformatics is difficult
% - bioinformaticians and not really scalable
% - people need our resources, we need to make them available

Bioinformatics is a rapidly growing field, but the growth in data is far outpacing the growth in researchers.
Advancements in sequencing mean that we generate NUMBER PB of data every TIME PERIOD which must be analysed.
Unfortunately researchers are not scaling at the same rate as the data.

It is a classic economics problem, the supply of humans to accomplish research is significantly lower than the demand.
The demand continues to grow putting pressure on the supply chain for bioinformaticians, pushing us to find ways to make
bioinformatics, and bioinformaticians, more scalable.

Naturally we look at this problem as a systems problem: what levers are available to us which can change the inputs or
outputs of the system to match our desired goal? We can turn the compute lever, making compute resources more available
to researchers via efforts like the \#EuroHPC JU. Or maybe we should place pressure on making tooling more cost
efficient. Can we increase the supply of students coming in to bioinformatics programs?

It is the last point where we have focused a number of our efforts: increasing and helping to accelerate the supply of
bioinformaticians that are needed around the world. We have made numerous training resources available, and then looked
at how to optimally scale these resources to reach the world.

\section{Scope of this Thesis}
In chapter \ref{chapter:platform} we present three highly integrated components as a necessary basis for the rest of the thesis. First a platform on which to scale one's research, from learning and courses to full fledge research. Into this platform a scalable and FAIR training platform has been integrated leveraging the connections to optimise the learning experience of students. And lastly, we address the needs of trainers using these two platform components by providing training infrastructure, as a service, reaching 24.000 researchers over the 5 years reported on as of the publication date.
% TODO: maybe I should move the GTN paper to chapter 2?

As the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic isolated the world, we were able to leverage the platform and training infrastructure to rapidly respond to the needs of the community, namely providing an e-learning platform. In chapter \ref{chapter:training} we show that the training platform, alongside Galaxy and TIaaS is scalable and functional even in the face of a sharp transition from in-person to e-learning. We show the platform scales to teach ~7.000 registrants over three years of online courses.

With a truly scalable, FAIR platform in place we next look at the connection to the medical research world, specifically cancer genomics in chapter \ref{chapter:cancer}. We demonstrate this by deploying a number of bioinformatics tools import for Cancer on our platform and making them available globally. This only left us with a problem of where to obtain data, thus necessitating the integration of the European Genome Archive (EGA) in a secure manner, allowing for end-to-end analysis of sensitive clinical data. Lastly we present a clinical application of this platform with the analysis of EC-PC data.

Lastly chapter \ref{chapter:annotation} we look at the annotation of genomes, a necessary step in the analysis of any genome, especially in medical contexts. We start by presenting a platform for scalable, real-time genome annotation, and then demonstrate the use of this platform in the annotation of phages, a topic of growing interest in the medical community. We also present the work of an ontology based toolkit for FAIR community databases, and summarise all of this infrastructure work by making our genome annotation infrastructure easily re-deployable by other groups via the GGA docker containers.
