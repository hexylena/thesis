\section{A Brief History} %<<<

The ``source code of life'' could finally begin to be unravelled for organisms, with the advent of DNA sequencing in the 1970s.
The first organism sequenced was bacteriophage $\phi$X174 (5386 nucleotides long) in 1976, which marked the start of the sequencing race.
The Human Genome Project (HGP) was launched in 1990, resulting in a complete copy of the Human genome being made available by HGP and Celera Genomics by 2003.
These scientific and technological developments paved the way for the era of bioinformatics, and for its subsequent application to translational research and clinical diagnostics.

A new field combining informatics\footnote{For non-European audiences, informatics traces its lineage from \textit{Informatica}, i.e. computer science and computational informatics, rather than simply a meaning like ``data'' or ``information''.} with biological research, Bioinformatics, evolved with genomic data availability.
This new interdisciplinary field would be built upon biology, computer science, chemistry, statistics, and more, leveraging knowledge from all of those fields to analyse (non-)human data.
Computers and algorithms were essential to the field by the 1980s,which resulted in a period of rapid developments across the field of genomics.
These included comparative genomics to enable multi-genome analysis, phylogenetics to identify the history of organisms, genome annotation methods to determine what DNA does, and the first genomic repositories providing worldwide access to genomic and sequencing data (genbank, embl)!
By a decade later in 1992, these public sequence repositories enabled the development of Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) which let researchers search for DNA sequences across ever-growing databases of genomes.

\subsection{Skill Gaps} %<<<

Workflows are a key product for a lot of bioinformaticians, where a scientist has combined a set of programs and their parameters into a reproducible and re-runnable unit of work. 
When bioinformaticians develop workflows, they need to run somewhere, either locally in the hospital, or in the cloud. These \textbf{infrastructure} resources are commonly experienced as difficult to access, requiring a completely different set of skills to ensure code runs efficiently on highly parallel distributed resource managers (DRMs), beyond typical programming-type tasks, moving into the field of software engineering.

General Data Protection Regular (GDPR)'s passage has further frustrated this, requiring researchers be more careful about data locality, access control, and developing an understanding of the \textbf{legalities} of processing clinical data in different situations. Both accidental exposures, and breaches of patient data due to hacking, have made hospitals wary about the security of the data they are responsible for maintaining. Thus, many hospitals mandate use of a prescribed platform for data analysis which often comes with additional barriers or costs to access. Researchers may need to be physically within the hospital to access the data, or forbidden from analysing it on large external clusters to which they might have access out of legal concerns. If they cannot analyse their data locally, they potentially need to find additional budget to pay for access to their employer's secure computation environment.

In addition to infrastructure challenges, \textbf{programs} or ``tools'' are run, often in a wide variety of languages, each with different requirements, different programming languages, and different databases. To run an individual tool and generate a clinically acceptable output requires the bioinformatician to understand deeply the process of each tool, the methods by which it functions, data types it can accept, and more. Occasionally the researcher will want to modify the tool to add a new feature, or support for a new organism, which requires the ability to write code in any of those various languages that might be employed. Using pipelines which includes software written multiple languages is not uncommon, requiring the researcher to know how to build and install and configure software from all of those ecosystems.

Computers themselves are notoriously user unfriendly themselves, so much so we have an entire field of research dedicated to user experience and human computer interaction. All of the infrastructure, tools, and data put in front of a busy doctor, or a researcher, needs to be clear, accessible, and \textbf{user-friendly}. This requires an investment of time and space in the development cycle to ensure all sharp edges are polished, and the software and research products are optimised for their clinical settings.

And all of the above challenges are compounded by the need to provide \textbf{training} materials. No matter how user friendly the systems are, they must still be documented in a variety of ways that everyone interacting with the system will require: documentation for developers, training for clinicians, reference manuals for failure modes. Also such that busy biologists or technicians can run and share the results with clinicians who must interpret the results in an efficient manner. The end users must be trained on system operation, and barriers eliminated in service of clinical outcomes. In the same vein, the short and long courses required to up-skill the aforementioned bioinformaticians must be developed, taught, and maintained.

An underlying theme in the above, while remaining a separate issue, is the \textbf{accessibility} of those resources. The learning materials, the infrastructure, the tooling, the legal knowledge; all of this must be accessible to the motivated bioinformatician attempting to resolve their skill issues. There are multiple forms of ``accessibility'' barriers that can be experienced, often conflated under the same word; inaccessibility due to lack of effort to make content and resources accessible to those with disabilities, inaccessibility due access restrictions like firewalls or paywalls, inaccessibility due missing metadata or (meta)data not using a standard protocol.

\subsection{Data Gaps} %<<<

On the data side we experience similar challenges related to insufficient time and resources allocated. Notably while we have a wealth of primary data products (e.g. DNA, RNA sequencing), the secondary, downstream data products are presenting a new limit.

Clinical pipelines are exceptional in their data requirements: in the clinical world accurate \textbf{genome annotation} is of paramount importance. This can be seen especially as we look towards reviving old practices such as bacteriophages to fight infections, those phages must have completely correct annotations before they are exposed to humans. Unlike small mRNA-based vaccines where the entire sequence is readable, bacteriophages are significantly larger with significantly more unknown regions due to both difficulties in sequencing and time required for annotation. Many healthcare providers are cautious about introducing organisms with unknown genes into a patient, one wants to ensure annotations are human-reviewed for accuracy.

The same applies for human data as well: before we fully understand the genetic underpinnings of a disease or condition, we need to have a well annotated genome, the sequencing alone is of course insufficient. With a well annotated genome, analysis can begin. In the precision medicine use case this means increasingly working with \textbf{sensitive} human datasets, the challenge becomes how to handle these datasets safely and securely. Long term data storage becomes an important question to answer to ensure documented traceable clinical analysis.
% sensitive data: bench lab scientists need fool-proof ways to access sensitive, secure data to reduce potential for negative outcomes.
% federated data: these researchers also want to look at cohorts from other institutions, other countries in a GDPR safe manner, so we need to enable that.

For population level studies common in oncological research, these datasets are also often no longer in-house, and instead must be obtained from \textbf{federated} sensitive data repositories. Given the context of the technical and legal problems mentioned above, these problems become significantly more difficult as we look to transferring human data across national boundaries.
For the bioinformatician wishing to analyse those datasets, it might additionally mean using federated compute resources to bring their computations directly to the data.

And all of these data analysis and generation pipelines, naturally, \emph{should} be \textbf{reproducible}. Other researchers must be able to reproduce results over time to ensure defensible and accurate scientific research.
Bioinformaticians from earlier eras were used to doing analysis on their own computers; ``works on my laptop'' is not sufficient for the clinic, the datasets are larger, and must be reproducible across research environments.

\section{Scope of this Thesis} %<<<
In \cref{chapter:annotation} we examine the question of bacteriophages; for education they present an interesting opportunity to improve bioinformatics and genome annotation education, for healthcare they offer an interesting target for phage therapy. We present a suite of tooling which builds on open source software from the Generic Model Organism Database (GMOD) community to develop a comprehensive platform supporting genome annotation of bacteriophages from education through to production. Additionally we discuss visualisation of genomes leveraging Circos for complex multi-dimensional data visualisation. All of the produced results are made available on the FAIR data analysis platform that is Galaxy.

Next in \cref{chapter:cancer} we look towards clinical applications investigating sequencing data collected from Endothelial cells and Pericytes to investigate their response to Tumour Necrosis Factor (TNF), paving the way to future implications for treating cancers involving those two cell lines.
This work is complemented by the development of a FAIR data retrieval pipeline enabling researchers to more seamlessly access sensitive data for their research projects and analyses.

Finally, with a lot of interesting results in the first two themes, we look at how to disseminate this work to a wider audience through training.
As we benefit at every step from the existing learning materials produced by educators around the world, we give back by building a scalable and FAIR training platform which has seen significant developments over the last two years in \cref{chapter:training}.
This is paired with the investigation of a significant problem facing trainers: obtaining and configuring infrastructure for training events or courses.
We examine potential options and discuss the implementation of a platform which enables access to training infrastructure and it's use by 24,000 students over the last 5 years.
