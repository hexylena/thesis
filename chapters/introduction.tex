\section{A Brief History} %<<<

The ``source code of life'' could finally begin to be unravelled for organisms, with the advent of DNA sequencing in the 1970s.
The first organism sequenced was bacteriophage $\phi$X174 (5386 nucleotides long) in 1976\cite{Sanger_1977}, which marked the start of the sequencing race.
The Human Genome Project (HGP) was launched in 1990\cite{HGP}, resulting in an over 90\% complete genome reference sequence being made available by the HGP and Celera Genomics by 2003.
These scientific and technological developments paved the way for the era of bioinformatics, and for its subsequent application to translational research and clinical diagnostics.

A new field combining informatics\footnote{For non-European audiences, informatics traces its lineage from \textit{Informatica}, i.e. computer science and computational informatics, rather than simply a meaning like ``data'' or ``information''.} with biological research, Bioinformatics, evolved with genomic data availability.
This new interdisciplinary field was built upon biology, computer science, chemistry, statistics, and more, leveraging knowledge from all of those fields to analyse (non-)human data.
Computers and algorithms were essential to the field by the 1980s, which resulted in a period of rapid developments\cite{Ouzounis_2003}.
These computational methods included comparative genomics to enable multi-sequence and multi-genome analysis\cite{Gibbs_1970,Wagner_1974,Higgins_1988}, phylogenetics to identify the history of organisms\cite{Neyman_1971,Felsenstein_1981}, prediction of gene locations\cite{Gribskov_1984,Guig__1992}, and genome annotation methods to determine their functionality\cite{Gribskov_1987,Del_age_1988}. Additionally, the first genomic repositories providing worldwide access to genomic and sequencing data (e.g. GenBank® from National Institutes of Health\cite{Bilofsky_1988} and the data library at European Molecular Biology Laboratory\cite{Rice_1993,Attwood_2011}) were established.
By a decade later in 1992, these public sequence repositories enabled the development of Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST)\cite{Altschul_1990} which let researchers search for DNA sequences across ever-growing databases of genomes.

The astronomical\footnote{The sequencing of these initial genomes cost approximately $\text{⅓}^{\text{rd}}$ as much as an \emph{average} NASA shuttle mission\cite{Pielke_2011}} cost of sequencing of the first human genome ($\approx$450 million USD), released by the Human Genome Project in 2003, led to the aim of developing cheaper technologies. This was perhaps best exemplified by the \$1,000 genome project\cite{Mardis_2006,Service_2006,Check_Hayden_2014} established only 3 years later, which had the ambitious goal of sequencing a full human genome for a mere \$1,000 USD.
Thus began the era of \emph{next-generation-} or \emph{high-throughput sequencing} and the development of multiple short-read sequencing technologies which rapidly accelerated the sequencing process, and decreased sequencing costs, anticipating a future of personalised genomics\cite{Voelkerding_2009,Mardis_2008}.
In 2019, the goal of the \$1,000 genome was achieved, and the average cost of sequencing a human genome first fell below this threshold\cite{genome-cost}.

By the mid-1990s, sequence analysis within Bioinformatics had entered basic clinical research\cite{Maojo_2003}, such as a database of \emph{TP53} mutations in tumour cell lines based on sequencing analyses, which the authors hoped could provide predictive markers of disease relapse as well as a potential tool for patient diagnosis\cite{Hainaut_1997}
% https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC264428/
This was followed during the 2000s by the advent of Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) databases\cite{Johnson_2009} and Genome Wide Association Study (GWAS) databases\cite{Klein_2005}; data that was only possible by virtue of the existence of the full, annotated human reference genome.
Researchers could begin to sequence small portions of their patients' genomes more regularly, and compare them against the reference to look for interesting mutations which might have given rise to the disease phenotypes\cite{Esplin_2014,Prokop_2018}.
By the 2010 the genetics of individual tumour cell lines was regularly used in the application of cancer treatment via drugs, thanks to more affordable sequencing and the development of new genomics based screening methods\cite{Hamburg_2010}.

In more recent years, clinical bioinformatics has been working towards precision medicine, whereby bioinformatic methods are used to tailor treatments to an individual patient, based on their genetics\cite{Phillips_2020}.
Bioinformatics becoming prevalent in medicine opens up interesting new avenues for research, but comes with familiar and novel challenges.
With the influx of sequenced individual patient genomes, a wide variety of issues surrounding these datasets emerge, such as the long-term storage of this data, the necessary development of new software to analyse the large amounts of data, as well as non-informatics concerns such as the legalities and ethics of data use, storage, and sharing with other researchers\cite{Manrai_2017}.

\section{Limiting Reagents}  %<<<

In the pathway to synthesise clinical solutions from reactants such as sequencing data and bioinformaticians, there are a number of key limiting reagents:

\begin{itemize}
	\itemsep0em
	\item Data Availability %  originally just 'data', YUNLEI: Data generation? Data quality? Data availability? Data storage? Data…?
	\item Compute Availability\footnote{E.g. Memory, CPU cores, Storage, etc. that are all required for data analysis.}
	\item Bioinformaticians
\end{itemize}

With the data availability solved\cite{Stephens_2015} by eliminating the economic barrier, the first limit on system throughput was removed.
%https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4494865/
Currently the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) maintained by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) stores nearly 0.75 Exabasepairs\footnote{this number equates to around 300,000,000 full human genomes, however SRA is of course full of non-\textit{homo sapiens} sequencing data.} of sequencing data alone\cite{sra-growth}, which represents only a fraction of the total sequencing completed, and has been growing 15\% year-over-year. This amount is equivalent to the full genome sequence of 4 million individuals, added each year.

While their data center sizes and information is harder to estimate, compute resources have similarly grown over time.
The three major cloud providers (Amazon's AWS, Google's GCP, and Microsoft's Azure) offer compute resources in a time- and cost-efficient manner, enabling researchers to access large amounts of compute quickly\cite{Dudley_2010}.
On the non-commercial side, universities have continually competed for the top supercomputer spot, the total computation power available growing exponentially\cite{Dallaire_1984,Meuer_2013}.
Between clouds and local high-performance compute (HPC) resources, a researcher can take advantage of terabytes of memory and days of computation time, with minimal delay.
Before the advent of these compute solutions, accessing this amount of computational power required submitting a grant application justifying the usage, ordering of the infrastructure, and waiting for delivery and installation, all of which can now be completed in minutes.

\subsection{Bioinformatics Gaps}  %<<<

Software accessibility is a key component in making bioinformatics itself both more accessible and more efficient overall\cite{Krampis_2022}.
% https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.2144/btn-2021-0060
Computers themselves are notoriously user-unfriendly themselves, so much so that an entire field of research exists, dedicated to user experience and human computer interaction\cite{lazar2017research}.
All of the infrastructure, tools, and data put in front of a busy doctor, or a researcher, needs to be clear, accessible, and user-friendly\cite{Crisan_2018,Tornheim_2019,Valenstein_2008,Hettinger_2017}.
% https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.3002213
This requires an investment of time and space in the development cycle to ensure all sharp edges are polished, and the software and research products are optimised for a clinical settings.
The gaps in software interface accessibility must be analysed and simplifications investigated to reduce barriers for both existing bioinformaticians and newcomers, e.g. through simplified interfaces\cite{Wratten_2021}.
% https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-021-01254-9

The European Union's General Data Protection Regular (GDPR)'s passage has further increased infrastructure access challenges, requiring organisations and researchers to be more careful about data locality and access control. Additionally it requires an understanding of the legalities of processing clinical data in different situations, leading to questions about how to easily access data without compromising security\cite{van_Kessel_2023}.
Hospitals tend to be very conservative in response to potential leaks of patient data, and will take onerous and expensive security measures to decrease risks, to avoid repeating situations such as the Deadelus Biologie breach of 500k patient records\cite{enisa-threat-landscape,Yuan_2019,health-data-breach}.

This gap between researchers and their data analysis needs to be explored, the technical issues separated from the skill issues, and both addressed.

Both of the above challenges are compounded by the need to provide training materials covering the topics.
No matter how user friendly the systems are, they must still be documented in a variety of ways that everyone interacting with the system will require: documentation for developers, training for clinicians, reference manuals for failure modes.
It must be possible to ensure that busy biologists or technicians can run and share the results with clinicians who must interpret the results in an efficient manner\cite{Kawamoto_2005}.
The end users must be trained on system operation, and barriers eliminated in service of clinical impacts.
In the same vein, the short and long courses required to up-skill the aforementioned bioinformaticians must be investigated, developed, taught, and maintained\cite{Attwood_2017,Pearson_2001}.
Ensuring the longevity of these materials likely requires adherence Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR) principles, but this will need to be see as learning materials gain FAIRness over time.

An underlying theme in the above, while remaining a separate issue, is the accessibility of the solutions that can be developed to the aforementioned issues.
The learning materials\cite{Crane_2023,journals/corr/abs-2105-00076}, the data\cite{Tenopir_2011}, the tooling, the legal knowledge; all of this must be \emph{accessible} to bioinformaticians.
There are multiple forms of ``accessibility'' barriers that can be experienced, often conflated under the same word; inaccessibility due to lack of effort to make content and resources accessible to those with disabilities, inaccessibility due to access restrictions like firewalls or paywalls, inaccessibility due to missing (meta)data or (meta)data not following standards\cite{Roche_2015}.
Accessibility issues abound and must be catalogued, examined, and appropriate remedies explored.

\subsection{Data Gaps} %<<<

On the data side similar challenges are experienced relating to insufficient time and resources allocated to their solution. Notably while there exists a wealth of primary data products (e.g. DNA, RNA sequencing), the secondary, downstream data products are a potential future limit. Unfortunately their root cause is often again lack of human bioinformaticians.

Clinical pipelines are exceptional in their data requirements: in the clinical world accurate genome annotation is of paramount importance\cite{Roy_2018}.
This can be seen especially looking towards reviving old practices such as bacteriophages to fight infections, those phages must have correctly annotated and fully known genomes before they are exposed to humans\cite{Nilsson_2014,Philipson_2018}.
Unlike e.g. small mRNA-based vaccines where the entire sequence is known, bacteriophages are significantly larger with more unknowns, both due to difficulties in sequencing and time required for human annotators to investigate every coding region.
Many healthcare providers are cautious about introducing organisms with unknown genes into a patient; one wants to ensure annotations are human-reviewed for accuracy\cite{Pirnay_2015}.
Finally, looking to the long term maintenance and update of this high quality curation, it is apparent that sustainability of these efforts and their funding is difficult\cite{Vaught_2011,Berardini_2015}. Any solutions which decrease barriers to entry and maintenance must be investigated to work towards sustainable practices.

The same applies to human data as well: before the genetic underpinnings of a disease or condition are fully understood, an annotated genome must be available, the sequencing alone is of course insufficient\cite{Steward_2017}.
In the precision medicine use case this means increasingly working with sensitive human annotation datasets, the challenge becomes how to handle these datasets safely and securely and in GDPR compliant manners\cite{Minssen_2020}.
Long term data storage and reproducibility additionally becomes an important question to answer to ensure documented traceable clinical analysis\cite{Huang_2012}.
What does it mean for patient care if an analysis isn't reproducible?

For population level studies common in oncological research, these datasets are also often no longer in-house, and instead must be obtained from federated sensitive data repositories\cite{Rambla_2022}.
Given the context of the technical and legal problems, these problems become significantly more difficult when looking at transferring human data across national boundaries.
For the bioinformatician wishing to analyse those datasets, it might additionally mean using federated compute resources to bring their computations directly to the data\cite{De_Brouwer_2021}.
Infrastructure to support safe and secure cross-national exploration of medical datasets must be investigated and solutions devised which enable federated data access whilst protecting privacy and ensuring security.

\section{Scope of this Thesis} %<<<
In \cref{chapter:annotation} we examine the question of bacteriophages; for education they present an interesting opportunity to improve bioinformatics and genome annotation education, for healthcare they offer an interesting target for phage therapy.
We present a suite of tooling which builds on open source software from the Generic Model Organism Database (GMOD) community to develop a comprehensive platform supporting genome annotation of bacteriophages from education through to production, and consider its utility as a platform for annotation curation and maintenance.
Additionally we explore visualisation of genomic annotation data leveraging Circos for complex multi-dimensional data visualisation, and explore a simplified user interface to a complicated tool that is more accessible for researchers.
All of the produced results are made available on the Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR) data analysis platform that is Galaxy, exploring heterogeneous computational resources and software in a transparent manner.

Next in \cref{chapter:clinic} we investigate clinical applications of sequencing data collected from Endothelial cells and Pericytes to investigate their response to Tumour Necrosis Factor (TNF), paving the way to future implications for treating cancers involving those two cell types.
This work is complemented by the development of a FAIR data retrieval pipeline enabling researchers to more seamlessly access sensitive data for their research projects and analyses, decreasing access barriers while simultaneously maintaining data security for seamless access of federated clinical data.

Finally, with a lot of interesting results in the first two themes, we explore potential solutions for dissemination, ensuring the longevity and sustainability of our work by sharing our techniques through training.
As we benefit at every step from the existing learning materials produced by educators around the world, we give back by building a scalable and FAIR training platform which has seen significant developments over the last two years in \cref{chapter:training}.
We examine potential improvements to the underlying platform, including increasing accessibility of training materials for all tools on the platform, acting as a force multiplier.
This is paired with a significant problem facing trainers: obtaining and configuring infrastructure for training events or courses.
Solutions and methods by which we can decrease barriers and enable re-usability are explored through the implementation of a platform which enables easier access to training infrastructure.
